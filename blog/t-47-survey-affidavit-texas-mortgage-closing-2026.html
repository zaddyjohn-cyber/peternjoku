T-47 Affidavit in Texas: When an Old Survey Works, When It Doesn't, and Who Pays (2026)

ClosingBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 24, 20266 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland. One small form shows up on almost every resale closing in Dallas-Fort Worth, and most buyers have never heard of it: the T-47 Residential Real Property Affidavit. When it works, it saves you $400–$700 on a new survey. When it's filled out carelessly, it can delay a closing in the last week.

If you search for "T-47," most of what comes up is filled-in copies of the form. Here's the lender's version: what the form does, what the TREC contract says about surveys, when a title company won't accept an old survey, and who ends up paying.

What is a T-47 affidavit in Texas?

The T-47 is a sworn statement the seller signs about an existing survey of the property. The seller confirms that since that survey was made, there have been no new improvements, fences, additions, or easements, or lists any that were added. The title company uses the affidavit to decide whether it can rely on the old survey to amend the survey exception on your title policy.

There's also a T-47.1, a declaration that does the same job without a notary. It is signed under penalty of perjury under Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code §132.001. Your title company will tell you which one it wants. Most DFW title companies accept either.

Why it matters: a standard Texas owner's policy excludes "discrepancies, conflicts, or shortages in area or boundary lines" unless you pay to remove that exception. To remove it, the title company needs a survey it trusts. An old survey plus a clean T-47 is the cheapest way to get there.

What are my survey options in the TREC contract?

Paragraph 6C of the standard TREC One to Four Family Residential Contract gives three choices. Your agent checks one when you write the offer:

TREC 6C optionWhat happensWho pays for a new surveyBest when
(1) Seller furnishes existing survey + T-47Seller delivers the old survey and affidavit within the days stated. If the title company or lender won't accept it, a new survey is ordered.Whoever the contract names in this option (Seller or Buyer)Most resale homes with no changes since the last survey
(2) Buyer obtains new surveyBuyer orders a fresh survey within the days statedBuyerOlder homes, acreage, pools or additions, or no existing survey
(3) Seller furnishes new surveySeller orders and pays for a fresh surveySellerBuyer's market leverage, which is common in DFW right now

With DFW inventory up and price cuts common this fall, I've seen more buyers negotiate option (3), or option (1) with the seller paying if the T-47 is rejected. It's a small concession that sellers often accept. My seller concessions guide covers the bigger levers.

When will a title company reject an old survey?

The title company is insuring boundaries, so anything that changed the footprint since the survey is a problem. These are the rejections I see most often in DFW:

  • New or relocated fence, especially a cedar fence replaced after a storm and moved a foot or two
  • Pool, patio cover, pergola, or outdoor kitchen added after the survey
  • Storage building or detached garage, which is common on Forney, Terrell, and Royse City lots
  • Room addition or enclosed garage
  • A new easement, such as a utility or drainage easement recorded after the survey
  • An unreadable or incomplete survey, or one that doesn't match the current legal description
  • A T-47 with the "changes" section left blank. Title companies tell me this is one of the most common reasons for a delay.

Some lenders and title companies also look at aerial imagery. If a shed or pool shows up that isn't on the survey, the old survey gets rejected and a new one is ordered, sometimes the week of closing.

How much does a survey or the survey amendment cost?

There are two separate costs, and buyers often confuse them:

For scale, a Texas title company's published example uses a $500,000 sale with a $2,940 basic owner's premium, which makes the amendment $147. On a typical DFW home it's less. I almost always recommend it: about $100–$150 to insure your boundary lines is one of the cheapest protections in the closing. Does my lender require a survey?

Usually, in practice. Most lenders I work with want the survey exception amended on the lender's title policy too, which means the title company needs an acceptable survey. A clean T-47 with the old survey satisfies that most of the time. If it doesn't, the new survey becomes a loan condition. My guide to conditional approval covers how that fits into underwriting.

New construction is different. The builder's title company normally provides a new survey or a plat-based form, so the T-47 question doesn't come up.

What happened to a Rowlett buyer whose T-47 was wrong?

Last spring I worked with a first-time buyer purchasing a $340,000 home in Rowlett, near Lake Ray Hubbard. The contract used option (1) with the seller furnishing a 2015 survey. The seller signed a T-47 saying "no changes." When I walked the listing photos against the survey, I saw a storage building and an extended side fence that weren't on the drawing.

We raised it in week one rather than week four. The seller amended the affidavit and the title company said it needed a new survey. Because the contract said the seller pays if the T-47 isn't accepted, the seller covered the $550 survey. It came back in five business days and the buyer closed on schedule. If that shed had surfaced on an aerial check three days before closing, the date would have slipped.

What should I do as a buyer?

  1. Ask for the survey early. Have your agent request the existing survey and T-47 in the first few days of the option period. See my option period guide.
  2. Compare it to the house. Walk the lot with the survey. Fences, sheds, pools, and patio covers should all be on it.
  3. Read the "changes" section. If it's blank, or says "none" while you can see a new fence, ask the seller to correct it.
  4. Negotiate who pays if it fails. In this market, it's reasonable to ask the seller.
  5. Buy the amendment. At about 5% of the basic premium, it's worth it.

Who pays for a new survey if the T-47 is rejected in Texas?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329). It depends on which box is checked in Paragraph 6C of the TREC contract. Under option (1), the contract names who pays if the existing survey and T-47 aren't accepted, and that can be the seller or the buyer. Under option (2) the buyer pays, and under option (3) the seller pays. In today's DFW market I often see buyers negotiate for the seller to pay. Without it, your owner's policy doesn't cover boundary-line discrepancies, encroachments, or overlapping improvements. That's a lot of protection for a small cost.
