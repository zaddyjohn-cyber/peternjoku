Can I Use Crypto for a Down Payment on a House in Texas? (2026 Rules, Paper Trail and Tax Math)

Down PaymentBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 19, 20268 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and a question I get more often from Frisco, McKinney and Plano tech workers than from anyone else is: "Can I use my crypto for the down payment?" The short answer in October 2026 is yes — if you sell it and document it. The longer answer is that headlines like "Fannie Mae now accepts Bitcoin" have convinced a lot of buyers that unsold coins count as a down payment. They don't. Not on a standard loan, not yet.

Here is where the rules actually stand, the paper trail an underwriter wants to see, the tax math nobody includes in these articles, and how the new crypto-pledge loans compare to simply selling. I'll use real DFW numbers at today's rates — Freddie Mac's survey put the 30-year fixed at 7.40% for the week of October 15, 2026.

What are the three ways crypto can show up in a mortgage in 2026?

2. Count unsold crypto as reservesDirected by FHFA on June 25, 2025 — not a finished Selling Guide rule I can use on an ordinary loanProposal stage: U.S.-regulated centralized exchange, risk adjustments, capsYou can't plan a purchase around it yet

Fannie Mae's September 2, 2026 Selling Guide update and Freddie Mac's Bulletin 2026-12 of the same date did not add new crypto language, so path 1 is still the one I use for nearly every buyer.

What paper trail does an underwriter need to accept crypto sale proceeds?

Underwriting is about tracing money from a source you can prove to the closing table. With crypto, that trail has more links than a paycheck, and every missing link becomes a condition. Here's what I ask for:

  1. Proof you owned the coins. Exchange account statements in your name showing the holdings before the sale. If the coins sat in a self-custody wallet, the transaction record moving them onto the exchange.
  2. The sale. The exchange's trade confirmation or statement showing the sale and the dollar amount.
  3. The transfer to your bank. The withdrawal from the exchange and the matching deposit on your bank statement.
  4. The dollars in a regulated U.S. institution, verified before closing — a bank or credit union statement.
  5. A letter of explanation for the large deposit, tying it back to items 1–3.

If the money moved through several wallets or exchanges, document every hop. If you can't prove where it came from, the underwriter can't use it.

When should I sell my crypto before buying a house?

Earlier than you think. Two reasons. First, prices move — I've watched a buyer's down payment shrink 12% between going under contract and closing week because they waited to sell. Second, earnest money: summaries of the Fannie Mae guideline exclude crypto proceeds from earnest money, and in Texas your earnest money and option fee are due within about three days of the contract's effective date. My recommendation is to sell what you need for the down payment, closing costs and reserves before you start writing offers, move it to your bank, and let it sit. Once the dollars have been in your account across two monthly statements, they're seasoned and the conversation with the underwriter gets much shorter.

How much tax will I owe if I sell crypto for a down payment?

The IRS treats crypto as property, so selling triggers a capital gain or loss. Coins held more than a year get long-term rates, which are lower than short-term rates on coins held a year or less. Texas has no state income tax, which helps. Here's an illustration — not tax advice, and you should run your own numbers with a CPA:

Plan the tax bill as part of the purchase. A tax payment due next April is cash leaving your account after closing, and I'd rather you not be short on reserves the first year in the house.

What does a $400,000 Frisco purchase look like using crypto?

Take a $400,000 home in Frisco with 10% down on a conventional loan:

  • Down payment: $40,000. Loan: $360,000.
  • Closing costs: roughly 2–3% of the price, $8,000–$12,000.
  • Principal and interest at 7.40%: about $2,493 a month.
  • Property tax: Collin County runs about 1.5%, so $6,000 a year or $500 a month.
  • Insurance: about $175 a month. PMI at 10% down: often $100–$180 depending on credit.
  • Estimated PITI: roughly $3,270–$3,350 a month.

Selling: you need about $50,000 of crypto proceeds to cover the down payment and closing costs, plus the tax on the gain.
Pledging: to borrow the $40,000 down payment against Bitcoin at about $2.50 per $1, you'd lock up roughly $100,000 of BTC, and you'd carry a second loan payment on top of the PITI above — and that payment counts in your debt-to-income ratio. You avoid the tax now, but you're borrowing 100% of the purchase, and if Bitcoin falls, your collateral cushion shrinks.

Does FHA, VA or USDA treat crypto differently?

I haven't seen a separate crypto path for government loans. In practice I treat crypto on an FHA, VA or USDA file like any other asset you sold: the money has to be in U.S. dollars at a regulated bank, and the source has to be fully documented. Government loans also have lower down payments — 3.5% on FHA and zero on VA and USDA — so the amount you need to sell is usually much smaller. Ask your lender how they handle it before you sell, because individual lenders can add their own rules.

A McKinney buyer who sold early

Earlier this year I worked with a software engineer buying in McKinney — 760 credit score, about $62,000 in Bitcoin held since 2022, target price around $430,000. He wanted to hold the coins until closing week "in case it ran." We sold $55,000 worth in month one instead, moved it to his checking account, and let it season while he house-hunted. When we went under contract the earnest money and option fee came out of his bank, the underwriter cleared the large deposit with one letter of explanation and the exchange statements, and he closed with a conventional loan at 10% down. Bitcoin happened to drop after he sold. He's not a crypto skeptic now — he just liked closing on time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use Bitcoin for a down payment on a house in Texas?

Yes, if you sell it first and document the sale. I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and Fannie Mae's Selling Guide section B3-4.1-04 lets the proceeds of a virtual currency sale be used for a down payment, closing costs and reserves once the crypto has been exchanged into U.S. dollars, the dollars sit in a regulated U.S. financial institution, and they're verified before closing. You also have to document that you owned the coins and that the conversion happened. Unsold coins in a wallet or exchange account do not count as your down payment. Does Fannie Mae count crypto as reserves without selling it in 2026?

Not as a finished guideline. I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and FHFA ordered Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac on June 25, 2025 to prepare proposals for counting crypto held on U.S.-regulated exchanges toward reserves without converting it to dollars. As of October 2026 I have not seen that turn into a Selling Guide rule that lets an ordinary loan count unsold coins, and Fannie's September 2026 Selling Guide update did not add crypto language. Until it does, I treat crypto as an asset you sell and document. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and I'll tell you the day that changes.

Can I use crypto for earnest money in Texas?

I wouldn't plan on it. I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and according to summaries of Fannie Mae's virtual currency guideline, sale proceeds are acceptable for the down payment, closing costs and reserves but earnest money is excluded. In Texas the earnest money and option fee are typically due to the title company and seller within three days of the contract's effective date, so you want ordinary seasoned bank funds ready for that. Sell crypto early if it's your main source of cash, and let the dollars sit in your bank account before you write an offer. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 to plan the timing.

Is a crypto-backed down payment loan a good idea?

Sometimes, but go in with your eyes open. I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and the pledge product that launched in 2026 pairs a regular Fannie Mae first mortgage with a second loan secured by Bitcoin, and reports say it needs about $2.50 of Bitcoin locked up for every $1 borrowed. You avoid selling and the capital gains tax that comes with it, but the second loan has its own payment that counts in your debt-to-income ratio, and your coins stay locked until the first mortgage is paid off or refinanced. For most DFW buyers with a meaningful gain, I run both versions — sell versus pledge — side by side. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and I'll do that math with you.
