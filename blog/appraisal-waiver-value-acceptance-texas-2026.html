Appraisal Waivers and Value Acceptance in Texas 2026: Who Gets One, Who Can't, and What It Costs You

AppraisalsBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 16, 20269 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and this is one of the most misunderstood items in a mortgage file. Buyers ask me to "get them an appraisal waiver" roughly once a week, as though it's something I can request. It isn't. And almost every page you'll find on this topic is still using terminology Fannie Mae retired — it dropped "appraisal waiver" from its Selling Guide in favor of value acceptance in announcement SEL-2025-07. Freddie Mac's equivalent has always been called ACE, for Automated Collateral Evaluation.

Let me also set an expectation up front: there is no new 2026 appraisal waiver rule. The current framework came out of 2025 — the terminology change and the eligibility expansions. What has changed in 2026 is how often waivers actually get granted, and that data is worth seeing. Plus there's one Texas rule that no competitor page puts front and center, and it determines whether a lot of my clients get a waiver at all.

Who actually decides whether I get an appraisal waiver?

The automated underwriting system does. When I submit your loan to Fannie Mae's Desktop Underwriter or Freddie Mac's Loan Product Advisor, the system evaluates your loan against its own database of property records and prior appraisals and returns one of three valuation requirements. Neither you nor I can request a particular outcome.

Two things follow from that, and they matter:

  1. You can't shop for a waiver. If one lender's system declines to offer value acceptance on your file, another lender submitting the same file to the same system will usually get the same answer.
  2. An offered waiver is not a guaranteed waiver. Lenders may require an appraisal anyway — on an unusual property, a rural property, or when something in the file doesn't sit right. That is the lender's right, and a good one will use it.

How often do appraisal waivers actually happen now?

Less often than during the refinance boom, and heavily concentrated in refinances. According to AEI Housing Center data cited by the Appraisal Institute:

  • The combined waiver share across Fannie and Freddie reached about 28% in March 2026, up from roughly 26% in February.
  • That's still well below the near-50% peak during the 2021 refinance boom.
  • Waivers cluster in refinances: roughly 52% of Freddie Mac no-cash-out refinances and about 48% of comparable Fannie Mae transactions used one in March 2026.
  • Property data collections — the hybrid middle tier — account for only about 2–3% of GSE transactions.

The practical read: if you're refinancing a standard suburban DFW home with decent equity, there's a real chance you skip the appraisal. If you're buying, assume you're getting one and be pleasantly surprised if you don't.

Why can't a Texas cash-out refinance ever get an appraisal waiver?

This is the section that nothing else ranking for this topic puts up front, and it's the one that costs Texas homeowners actual money when they budget a refinance.

On a Texas Section 50(a)(6) home equity or cash-out refinance, value acceptance is not available and a new full appraisal is required — regardless of what Desktop Underwriter returns. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide section B5-4.1-01 requires that the fair market value be based on an appraisal, and that the appraisal be attached to the written acknowledgment of fair market value that you sign at closing. Lender product matrices for Texas 50(a)(6) loans say it even more bluntly: a full interior-and-exterior appraisal is required regardless of the appraisal format DU recommends, and property inspection alternatives and waivers are not permitted.

This is a direct consequence of the Texas Constitution building the appraisal into the consumer protections around home equity lending. It sits alongside the other 50(a)(6) rules Texas homeowners should know: an 80% maximum loan-to-value on your homestead, a required notice followed by a 12-day waiting period before closing, and fee caps.

A Texas rate-and-term refinance is different. If you're refinancing to change your rate or term without taking cash out, value acceptance and the reduced-scope options are genuinely on the table. If you have a 50(a)(6) loan already and want out of it, that's a 50(f)(2) refinance and it has its own rules worth understanding.

What about FHA, VA and USDA loans?

Value acceptance and ACE are conventional-only programs. The government loans run separate valuation systems:

  • FHA: an appraisal is required on a purchase. The appraisal is valid for 180 days from its effective date under Mortgagee Letter 2022-11, extendable to a year with an appraisal update. Critically, the value attaches to the FHA case number, not to the lender — so if you switch lenders but keep the same case number, the existing value travels with you; a new case number generally means a new appraisal. My post on what happens when an appraisal comes in low covers this in depth.
  • VA: values come from a Notice of Value, typically good for six months, and the VA has its own Tidewater process when an appraiser anticipates a value below the contract price.
  • USDA: an appraisal is required on a purchase.
  • Streamline refinances: the FHA Streamline and the VA IRRRL often skip the appraisal entirely — but that's a program feature, not value acceptance, and the eligibility rules are completely different.

What else makes a property ineligible?

Even on a conventional loan, these commonly knock out a waiver:

  • Properties valued at roughly $1 million or more
  • Manufactured homes
  • Two-to-four unit properties
  • New construction
  • Unique or hard-to-comp properties — acreage, heavily customized homes, anything without recent nearby sales
  • Gift-of-equity and certain non-arm's-length transactions
  • Texas 50(a)(6) cash-out, as above

That list hits parts of my market harder than others. A tract home in Plano or Allen is a strong waiver candidate. Five acres outside Terrell with a custom build is not.

Should I take the waiver, and what does it cost me if I'm wrong?

On a refinance, take it. You already own the house, you know what it's worth, and you're saving $550–$800 and a week.

On a purchase, think harder. The appraisal is the only independent check that you aren't overpaying. DFW prices have been running roughly flat to slightly down year over year in 2026, which means the market is no longer bailing out an overpayment the way it did in 2021. Here's the math on being wrong by $10,000 on a $350,000 purchase at Freddie Mac's current 7.40% benchmark:

If you overpay by…Added to your loanAdded monthly paymentTotal paid over 30 years
$5,000$5,000$35$12,463

So a waiver saves you $600 and ten days; overpaying by $10,000 costs you about $24,900 across the loan. That asymmetry is the whole argument. It doesn't mean refuse the waiver — it means keep your other protections. In Texas that's your option period, a separately paid, unrestricted right to terminate, plus a full inspection. Never give those up because a waiver made you feel confident about value. An inspection tells you about the roof and the foundation; an appraisal tells you about the price. They are not substitutes for each other.

Do I qualify for value acceptance?

The profile that gets offered one most often looks like this:

  • Conventional loan (not FHA, VA or USDA)
  • Refinance rather than purchase — and not a Texas 50(a)(6) cash-out
  • Lower loan-to-value — more equity means less risk to the investor
  • Standard single-family property in a well-documented subdivision with recent nearby sales
  • Strong credit and clean file — the system weighs overall risk, not just the property
  • The property already exists in the GSE's database from a prior transaction

Two real examples from this year. A couple in Plano refinanced a 2016-built home in a large subdivision — about 62% loan-to-value, scores in the 760s, rate-and-term with no cash out. Desktop Underwriter offered value acceptance on the first submission. They saved $625 and closed in 18 days instead of 28.

Same month, a homeowner in Rockwall wanted $45,000 out of a home worth about $420,000 to pay off a truck and consolidate two cards. Similar credit, similar equity position, and he had read somewhere that strong borrowers get appraisal waivers — he had budgeted his closing costs without one. I had to tell him his transaction was a Texas 50(a)(6) cash-out, which means a full appraisal is required by rule and no automated system will waive it. We added $650 to his costs and five days to his timeline. He still closed and still came out ahead, but it was an avoidable surprise, and the only reason it surprised him is that nobody writing about appraisal waivers mentions the Texas rule.

If you're buying, plan and budget for a full appraisal, and treat a waiver as a bonus rather than a strategy. Either way, I'll tell you straight what your file is likely to get instead of letting you budget on a guess.
