Wire Fraud at Closing in Texas: How to Protect Your Down Payment in 2026

ClosingBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 20, 20268 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and the most expensive mistake I've seen a DFW buyer almost make had nothing to do with rates or credit. It was a single email, two days before closing, with "updated wiring instructions" from what looked exactly like the title company. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) counted 12,368 real estate fraud victims and about $275 million in losses in 2025, up from 9,359 victims and about $173 million in 2024 — roughly a 59% jump in dollars lost in one year. And CertifID's 2026 State of Wire Fraud report found that about 1 in 5 homebuyers received a suspicious or fraudulent communication during their closing.

Most of what ranks for this topic is a generic national checklist from a lender or title underwriter. This post is the Texas version: how money actually moves at a Texas closing, where the scam slips in, what I do on my side, and the exact steps to take in the first hour if you think you've wired money to the wrong place.

How does money move at a Texas home closing?

In Texas, the title company (acting as escrow agent) handles the money. There are three separate flows, and knowing them makes a fake email easier to spot:

MoneyWho sends itWhere it goesWhen
Earnest money and option feeYouThe title company (escrow agent)Within 3 days of the effective date under the current TREC contract
Cash to close (down payment + closing costs)YouThe title company's escrow accountUsually 1–2 business days before closing
Loan fundsYour lenderDirectly to the title companyAt funding — you never touch it

Notice what's not on that list: your loan officer never asks you to wire money to them, and nobody legitimate asks you to wire money to an individual's personal account. The amount you send is the "cash to close" on your Closing Disclosure, which you get at least three business days before closing — so you know the number before any wiring email shows up.

What does a closing wire fraud email look like?

The pattern in nearly every case I've heard about is the same. Someone gets into an email account somewhere in the transaction — a real estate agent's, a title officer's, sometimes the buyer's — and reads the thread quietly for weeks. When closing gets close, they send an email from that account, or from a look-alike address that's one letter off, saying the wiring instructions have changed. The warning signs:

  • "Updated" or "corrected" wiring instructions — legitimate title companies almost never change their escrow account in the middle of a transaction.
  • Urgency. "Wire today or the closing will be delayed" or "the seller may cancel."
  • A request to keep it quiet or not call because "the office is closed for training."
  • A slightly wrong email address or domain — an extra letter, a ".co" instead of ".com", a free email service.
  • A phone number inside the email to "verify" — that number goes to the scammer.
  • A change of company mid-deal — a title office change, a builder acquisition, a new escrow officer. Any reshuffle is a natural cover story.

How do I verify wiring instructions before I send my down payment?

This is the routine I give every buyer at pre-approval, not in closing week:

  1. Get the title company's phone number yourself — from the title company's own website, from the contract your agent sent at the start, or from my office. Write it down now, before anyone has a reason to fake it.
  2. Call that number and ask the escrow officer to read the account and routing numbers to you. Compare digit by digit. Don't read your numbers to them; have them read theirs to you.
  3. Treat any change as fraud until proven otherwise. If instructions change, stop and call again at the number you looked up.
  4. Ask about alternatives. Some Texas title companies will accept a cashier's check for cash to close or offer a secure verified payment portal. Ask your escrow officer what they accept and how far ahead they need it.
  5. Send a small confirmation, then call. After the wire goes out, call the title company to confirm receipt the same day. Don't wait until the closing table.

Your bank can help too. Tell the teller or the wire desk the money is for a home closing and ask them to confirm the receiving bank name matches the title company's bank. A mismatch — for example, the beneficiary name is a person, not "XYZ Title Company Escrow Account" — is a hard stop.

What should I do if I wired money to a scammer?

Speed is everything. The first hours decide whether money can be frozen. Do these in this order:

  1. Call your bank's fraud or wire department immediately and ask them to recall the wire and contact the receiving bank. Ask for a reference number.
  2. File a complaint at ic3.gov right away. The FBI's Recovery Asset Team works with banks to freeze funds. In one case the National Association of Realtors described this year, a couple wired more than $449,000 after an email impersonating their attorneys, and IC3 was able to get the receiving account frozen with the full amount on hold.
  3. Call your local police department and get a report number.
  4. Tell your title company, your agent and me — by phone. Assume the email thread is compromised.

Here's the hard part: in most cases the buyer still owes the cash to close. Your owner's title policy protects your ownership of the house; it is not insurance against you sending your own money to a criminal. That's why prevention matters so much more than recovery.

A Rowlett buyer who almost wired $21,400 to the wrong account

A buyer I worked with on an FHA purchase in Rowlett — first home, about $21,400 cash to close — got an email two days before closing from what looked like her escrow officer. Same name, same signature block, same logo. It said the title company was "switching banks for end-of-month reconciliation" and attached new wiring instructions. The only tell was the domain: one letter was doubled.

She almost didn't notice. What saved her was a sticky note on her refrigerator from our first meeting with the title company's main number on it. She called, the escrow officer said they had never sent that email, and the title company alerted everyone in the transaction. She wired to the correct account the next morning, confirmed receipt by phone, and closed on time. The scam email had come from an account that had clearly been reading the thread — it referenced her closing time.

What do I do as your loan officer to protect your closing?

  • I will never email you wiring instructions, and I tell you that in writing at the start.
  • I give you the title company's verified phone number in your pre-approval package.
  • Your loan funds go straight from the lender to the title company — they never pass through you or me.
  • If anything changes in your transaction — a new title office, a new escrow officer, a builder acquisition — I call you, and I don't send new instructions by email.

Wire safety is one piece of a clean closing. Does title insurance cover wire fraud at closing?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and generally no — your owner's title policy protects your ownership of the property against title defects, not money you send to a criminal before closing. Some title companies carry their own crime or cyber coverage, but that protects the company, and you should not count on it covering your loss. Prevention is your real protection: verify by phone, and confirm receipt the same day. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 if you want me to walk through your closing-day steps.

Can I pay my closing costs with a cashier's check instead of a wire in Texas?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and some Texas title companies accept a cashier's check for cash to close, while others require a wire or use a secure verified payment portal, and many have limits or need the check in advance. Ask your escrow officer early what they accept and how far ahead they need it. Whatever method you use, confirm the instructions by phone at a number you looked up yourself.
