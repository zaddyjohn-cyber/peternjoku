Mortgage Interest Deduction Texas 2026: When It Makes Sense to Itemize vs Take the Standard Deduction

Homeowner FinancesBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 15, 20267 min read

This post discusses tax rules and their interaction with mortgage decisions. I'm a licensed Mortgage Loan Officer, not a CPA or tax advisor. Please verify your specific tax situation with a licensed tax professional before making tax filing decisions.

One question I get from buyers and homeowners all the time: "Is the mortgage interest deduction still worth something in 2026?" The honest answer is: it depends on your loan size — and Texas has a specific math that's different from most other states.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a licensed Mortgage Loan Officer (NMLS #2670329) based in Garland. I'm not a CPA, and I can't give you personal tax advice, but I can help you understand how the deduction works in the context of a Texas mortgage — and why the 2025 tax law changes matter for DFW homeowners in ways most people haven't had explained to them.

The 2026 numbers you need: standard deduction and SALT cap

Two numbers govern whether itemizing makes sense in 2026:

  • Standard deduction (married filing jointly): $32,200
  • Standard deduction (single filer): $16,100
  • SALT cap (State and Local Tax) for 2026: $40,400 MFJ / $20,200 single

The SALT cap was significantly increased under the One Big Beautiful Budget Act (OBBBA) signed into law in 2025, effective for tax years 2025–2029. It replaced the previous $10,000 cap that was widely criticized. The new limit is $40,400 for married filers — which sounds like a lot, but for most Texas homeowners, it's actually not the binding constraint. Here's why:

Texas has no state income tax. Your entire SALT deduction is made up of property taxes. Most DFW homeowners pay $7,000–$15,000 per year in property taxes depending on county and home value. That's well under the $40,400 cap — so the SALT cap change, while positive for Texas homeowners in theory, mostly doesn't change the math for them. What matters is whether mortgage interest + property taxes clears the standard deduction.

The mortgage interest deduction in 2026: what qualifies

  • Acquisition debt cap: You can deduct interest on up to $750,000 of mortgage debt used to buy, build, or substantially improve your primary home (and one second home). If your mortgage exceeds $750,000, you deduct only the proportional share of interest.
  • Home equity interest: Interest on a HELOC or home equity loan is deductible only if the funds were used to buy, build, or improve the home that secures the loan — not for debt consolidation or other purposes.
  • PMI deductibility: Mortgage insurance premiums (PMI on conventional, MIP on FHA) are deductible again in 2026 as an itemized deduction, subject to an income phase-out beginning at $100,000 adjusted gross income.
  • 1098 form: Your lender sends a Form 1098 each January showing total mortgage interest paid. This is the number you give your tax preparer.

What this means for DFW buyers in 2026

The general rule in Texas: if your mortgage is under $350,000–$375,000, you're probably better off taking the standard deduction. If your mortgage is above $400,000, you should be itemizing — and the benefit increases as your loan and property tax amounts grow.

This is different from how it worked pre-2018 (before the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act raised the standard deduction), and it's different from high-SALT states like California or New York where itemizing is almost always beneficial. Texas homeowners operate in a unique environment: no state income tax means your SALT is only property taxes, and the SALT cap increase mainly helps very high-income homeowners in high-tax states — not the average DFW buyer.

The Plano example: $450,000 loan in Collin County

Consider a married couple in Plano with a $450,000 mortgage on a $500,000 home, purchased with a conventional loan at 7.40%. Collin County effective property tax rate is approximately 1.7%:

  • Year 1 mortgage interest: ~$32,850
  • Collin County property tax (1.7% × $500K): ~$8,500
  • PMI: $0 (10% down, no PMI at 90% LTV with some conventional programs)
  • Total itemized: ~$41,350
  • Standard deduction MFJ: $32,200
  • Itemized saves: ~$9,150 in deductions

If they're in the 22% federal bracket, that $9,150 in extra deductions is worth approximately $2,013 per year in actual tax savings. Not transformative — but real money.

High-rate environment advantage: more interest to deduct

Here's something counterintuitive: at 7.40%, your first-year interest payments are substantially larger than they would be if rates fell to 5.5%. A higher rate means a bigger potential itemized deduction — right now. If you refinance later at 5.5%, your interest deduction shrinks, but you're also paying less interest total. The point isn't to stay at 7.40% for the tax benefit — it's that the tax benefit is at its maximum value right now, not later.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329) — yes, Texas homeowners can still deduct mortgage interest on federal income taxes in 2026, but only if total itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction. Because Texas has no state income tax, your SALT is limited to property taxes — most DFW homeowners pay $7,000–$15,000 in property tax per year. Is PMI deductible in 2026 in Texas?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329) — yes, PMI on conventional loans and MIP on FHA loans are deductible again in 2026. The deduction phases out starting at $100,000 adjusted gross income and is fully phased out at $110,000. If you're paying PMI and your income is under $100,000, this adds to your itemized total. If your income is above $110,000, you receive no PMI deduction. Always confirm your specific situation with a licensed CPA. The deduction supplements the financial case for ownership; it doesn't change whether the purchase itself makes sense.
