How to Find Good Neighbor Next Door Homes in DFW Texas (2026)

Homebuyer ProgramsBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 8, 202610 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and the Good Neighbor Next Door program is the best homebuying benefit in the country that almost nobody successfully uses. A 50% discount off the list price, for teachers, firefighters, EMTs and police officers. HUD runs it, it's been around for years, and it's genuinely that good.

The reason so few DFW buyers use it isn't eligibility. It's that every article explaining the program stops exactly where the hard part begins: where are the houses, and how do I actually bid on one?

HUD's own page explains the rules. Bankrate explains the concept. The one substantive Texas article I could find on it is written in Spanish. Nobody has written the practical version for DFW. So here it is — including the two things that stop most people, and an honest assessment of what you'll find when you search.

What is Good Neighbor Next Door, and who qualifies?

GNND lets eligible public servants buy certain HUD-owned homes at 50% off the list price, in exchange for living there as their primary residence for 36 consecutive months.

The eligible occupations are a closed list of four, and the requirements are specific:

OccupationEmployment requirementJurisdictional tie
TeacherFull time, state-accredited public or private school, pre-K through grade 12The school must serve the area where the home is located
FirefighterFull time, with a fire department serving the areaMust serve the jurisdiction containing the home
Emergency medical technicianFull time, with an EMS unit serving the areaMust serve the jurisdiction containing the home
Law enforcement officerFull time, sworn, with a government or tribal agencyMust have arrest powers in the area containing the home

Beyond the occupation, you must not have owned a home as your primary residence in the past twelve months, and you must certify you'll occupy the home for 36 months. First-time buyer status is not required.

A correction I give out weekly: nurses are not eligible. People confuse GNND with "Nurse Next Door," which is a privately operated commercial program with no connection to HUD and nothing like these terms. If you're a DFW nurse, your better route is almost always FHA combined with TSAHC or TDHCA assistance — see my down payment assistance page.

How does the 50% discount work on the loan side?

This is the mechanic that surprises everyone, so let me be precise, because misunderstanding it leads people to bid wrong.

You bid the full list price. HUD does not negotiate and does not accept a discounted offer. On a $167,000 listing, your broker submits $167,000, and you are awarded the home at $167,000. HUD then takes back a silent second mortgage for 50% of the list price — $83,500 — which carries no interest and no monthly payment, and is forgiven in equal portions across your 36 months of occupancy.

So your first mortgage — the loan you actually qualify for and pay on — is based on the remaining $83,500. If you move out or sell before 36 months, you repay the unforgiven prorated share of that second.

Which homes qualify, and where are they in DFW?

Only HUD real-estate-owned (REO) homes located in designated revitalization areas are GNND-eligible. That's a narrow pool, and it's the reason the program is underused rather than oversubscribed. These are homes HUD acquired after an FHA loan foreclosed, in census tracts HUD has designated for revitalization.

In DFW, GNND listings have historically appeared in:

  • Garland — older neighborhoods in the central and southern parts of the city
  • Mesquite — established areas with a high concentration of entry-level housing stock
  • South and southeast Dallas — the largest share of DFW revitalization tracts
  • Fort Worth — east and southeast sides

I want to set expectations honestly, because the pages that oversell this program waste people's time. Revitalization-area designations change, and DFW GNND inventory is thin and intermittent. You may check for a month and see nothing. Then two listings appear in the same week. That pattern is exactly why preparation beats searching — each listing has a short window, and buyers who aren't already pre-approved miss it.

How do I search HUDHomeStore for GNND listings?

GNND homes are not on Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com or the MLS. They're only on HUD's own portal. Here's the walkthrough:

  1. Go to HUDHomeStore.gov — HUD's official listing portal for its REO inventory. Ignore lookalike sites; several lead-generation operations use similar names.
  2. Register a free account. You don't need one to browse, but you will need one, and your broker will need their HUD registration, to bid.
  3. Set the state to Texas and narrow by county — Dallas and Tarrant are your highest-probability counties — or by ZIP code if you need a specific school attendance zone.
  4. Filter for the Good Neighbor Next Door program. This is essential. Most HUD listings are ordinary REO homes open to any buyer; only the GNND-flagged ones carry the 50% discount.
  5. Check the listing period on each result. GNND properties are posted for a short exclusive window — commonly described as seven days. Window rules have changed over the years, so read the dates on the actual listing rather than relying on any article, including this one.
  6. Open the property details for the list price, the condition disclosures and the inspection report. HUD sells strictly as-is.
  7. Contact a HUD-registered broker immediately if something fits. Which brings us to the real obstacle.

Why can't I bid on a HUD home myself?

Because HUD doesn't let you. Bids must be submitted electronically by a real estate broker registered with HUD, on your behalf. There is no self-service path, however well qualified you are.

This single requirement is, in my experience, what defeats most eligible buyers. They find a listing, they're ready, and then they lose three days of a seven-day window locating a broker who is actually HUD-registered — because most agents aren't, and some will say yes before checking. HUD maintains a broker search on its site; use it rather than assuming.

Here's how the rest of the process runs:

StepWhat happensTiming
Listing postedProperty appears on HUDHomeStore flagged for GNNDShort exclusive window — commonly 7 days
Bid submittedYour HUD-registered broker bids electronically at the full list priceBefore the window closes
Multiple biddersHUD selects the winner by random lottery — not highest bid, not first inGenerally ~2 business days after the window closes
Earnest moneyDue by cashier's check, certified check or money orderGenerally within ~2 business days of winning
Financing and closingYour lender underwrites the first mortgage; HUD records the silent secondTypically 30–45 days
Occupancy period36 consecutive months as your primary residenceBegins at closing

The lottery detail is worth absorbing: you cannot outbid anyone. Everyone bids list price, and ties go to chance. Your job is to be eligible, pre-approved, and submitted on time — nothing else improves your odds.

What does an FHA loan on a GNND home actually cost per month?

Let's price a realistic DFW HUD home: $167,000 list price in Garland, financed with FHA at 3.5% down.

ItemAmount
List price (your bid)$167,000
HUD silent second (50%, forgiven over 36 months)−$83,500
Your financed basis$83,500
FHA down payment at 3.5%$2,922
Base loan amount$80,578
Plus FHA upfront MIP at 1.75%$1,410
Total loan amount$81,988
Principal & interest at 7.0%, 30 years$545
FHA annual mortgage insurance (0.55%)$37
Property taxes — Dallas County ~2.0% on the full $167,000$278
Homeowners insurance (estimated)$133
Total monthly payment (PITI + MI)~$993

Note the tax line carefully, because it's a wrinkle no one warns about: your property taxes are assessed on the full value of the home, not the discounted half. The appraisal district doesn't care about HUD's silent second. So your taxes are $278 a month on a house you financed $83,500 of — which is why taxes are more than half your principal and interest here. I cover that effect in detail in my post on how Texas property taxes affect your DTI.

Even so: under $1,000 a month, all in, for a three-bedroom home in Garland, with $2,922 out of pocket. That is well below what the same house rents for.

A real DFW example: a Garland elementary teacher

Let me describe a composite of the eligible buyers I've worked with, because the pattern is consistent.

A fourth-grade teacher at a Garland ISD campus, eight years in, earning $58,000, with a 681 credit score and about $6,000 saved. She'd been renting a two-bedroom in Garland for $1,595 a month and had concluded she couldn't buy, which on a conventional 5%-down loan at Garland's median price was accurate.

She qualified for GNND on all counts — full-time, state-accredited, pre-K-through-12, teaching at a school serving the area where she wanted to buy. We did three things in the right order. I got her FHA pre-approval done up front, so she wasn't scrambling later. She located a HUD-registered broker and confirmed the registration directly. Then she checked HUDHomeStore weekly, filtered to Texas and GNND.

It took eleven weeks before anything appeared. When a $167,000 three-bedroom in a south Garland revitalization tract posted, her broker bid the full list price inside the window. She was the only eligible bidder, so no lottery was needed. She closed with $2,922 down plus about $3,400 in closing costs, at a payment near $993 a month — roughly $600 a month less than her rent — and $83,500 of her purchase price will be fully forgiven in month 36.

The eleven weeks is the part I want you to take seriously. The buyers who succeed at this treat it as a standing search with financing already in place, not a project they start when they see a listing.

What if there are no GNND listings in my area?

This is the most likely outcome on any given week, and it's where every other article leaves you stranded. Three things to do:

Keep the search running and stay pre-approved. Check weekly. HUD REO inventory refreshes as foreclosures complete and move through HUD's disposition process, so an empty search today says nothing about next month. Keep your pre-approval current — most are good for 90 days and easy to refresh.

Widen the geography if you can. Your jurisdictional tie limits you, but it may be broader than you assume — a Dallas ISD teacher or a Dallas County deputy has a wide eligible area. Check Dallas and Tarrant counties both.

Then use the programs that are actually available now. For most DFW public servants, this is the realistic path, and the combined benefit is substantial:

Dallas County's DPA income limit is about $97,200, and a Garland teacher at $58,000 clears it comfortably. For many of my clients, FHA stacked with a TSAHC grant produces a smaller down payment than GNND would have — without the 36-month occupancy commitment or the wait for inventory. How do I get started?

  1. Confirm your eligibility against the occupation table above, including the jurisdictional tie.
  2. Get pre-approved first. You cannot bid without it, and the windows are too short to arrange financing afterward.
  3. Find and verify a HUD-registered broker before you need one. Confirm the registration; don't take a yes at face value.
  4. Register on HUDHomeStore.gov and set up a weekly Texas + GNND search.
  5. Budget for condition. HUD sells as-is, and these are often older homes needing work. If a property needs more than cosmetics, ask me about FHA 203(k) renovation financing, which can roll repairs into the loan.
  6. Run the parallel track. Apply for TSAHC or TDHCA assistance while you search, so you have a live option either way.
