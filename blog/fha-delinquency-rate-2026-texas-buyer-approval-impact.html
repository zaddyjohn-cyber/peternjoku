FHA Delinquencies Hit 11.79% in 2026: What That Actually Means If You're Buying With FHA in Texas

FHA LoansBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 16, 20269 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and if you've been researching FHA loans you've probably run into a headline about rising FHA delinquencies. A lot of what's written about it reads like a disaster movie — "the coming wave," "a gathering storm," one site advertising that FHA is "6.3x" conventional. Meanwhile the trade publications report the numbers accurately but write for lenders, not for the family in Mesquite trying to figure out whether this affects them.

So here is the calm version, with the real numbers and the actual answer to the question you're asking: does this change my approval?

The short answer is no, and I can show you the data behind that. The longer answer is that the delinquency figures point to something you genuinely should plan around — not your approval, but your cushion after closing. Let's take both.

What do the FHA delinquency numbers actually say?

These come from the Mortgage Bankers Association's National Delinquency Survey for the second quarter of 2026, released August 13, 2026. Note the quarter — the Q3 figures normally publish in November, so this is the current reading as of mid-October.

Loan typeTotal delinquency (Q2 2026)Change from Q1Change from a year earlierSerious delinquency change YoY
FHA11.79%Down 9 bps (from 11.88%)Up 122 bpsUp more than 225 bps
VA4.89%Down 10 bpsUp 57 bpsUp about 31 bps
Conventional2.72%Up 12 bpsModestly higherUp about 6 bps

A few honest observations about that table that the alarming headlines skip:

  • Total FHA delinquency went down quarter over quarter, not up. It fell 9 basis points from Q1's 11.88%.
  • It is up year over year — 122 basis points — so the trend is genuinely worse than 2025.
  • Serious delinquency is the real concern. Loans 90+ days past due or in foreclosure rose more than 225 basis points year over year on FHA, versus about 6 on conventional. That's where the stress is concentrated, and it's a meaningful signal.
  • FHA at 11.79% against conventional at 2.72% is a ratio of about 4.3 times — not the 6.3x some aggregator sites advertise. Do the division yourself; the number they're selling doesn't match the data they cite.

Why does FHA run so much higher than conventional?

Because FHA is doing a harder job on purpose, and the statistic reflects the design rather than a defect.

FHA exists so that a buyer with a 580 credit score and 3.5% down can buy a house. That's the whole point of the program, and it's why I've been able to get first-time buyers into homes in Garland, Mesquite and South Dallas who would never clear conventional underwriting. But a borrower who puts 3.5% down starts with 3.5% equity and, usually, modest savings. A conventional borrower putting 10–20% down starts with more of both. When a water heater fails or a job changes, the second borrower has more room.

So a higher FHA delinquency rate isn't evidence that FHA borrowers are irresponsible. It's the measurable cost of a program that trades a thinner cushion for access. Understanding that lets you do the one thing that matters: build the cushion yourself.

Does this make my FHA approval harder?

Here is the data point that answers it directly, and I haven't seen anyone else pair these two things.

On October 8, 2026 the MBA published its Mortgage Credit Availability Index for September. The Government component — FHA, VA and USDA — was completely unchanged. The Conventional component fell 0.4%, with lenders tightening documentation and pulling back on cash-out and investor programs. In other words: at the same moment FHA delinquency is elevated, FHA credit availability did not tighten at all. I wrote about that index in detail here.

What you may run into is lender overlays — extra requirements an individual lender stacks on top of FHA's published rules. Common overlays:

  • A minimum credit score above FHA's 580 (many lenders won't go below 620 or 640 regardless of what FHA allows)
  • A tighter debt-to-income ceiling than FHA's automated system would approve
  • Required reserves on a single-family purchase, where FHA itself requires none
  • Stricter treatment of recent late payments, collections or disputed accounts

Overlays vary enormously between lenders and investors, which is the practical reason it matters who works your file. A 585-score borrower who gets declined is often declined by an overlay, not by FHA — and I know which investors will actually lend at that score.

Is FHA itself in trouble?

No, and this is the context the doom coverage leaves out entirely.

FHA's Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund — the reserve that backs every FHA loan in the country — reached a record of roughly $189 billion with a capital ratio around 11.45%. Congress requires a 2% minimum, so the fund sits at more than five times its statutory floor. FHA's own stress testing projected the ratio falling to about 4.42% in a downturn on the scale of the Great Recession — still comfortably above the minimum.

And FHA's actual policy direction in 2026 has been toward expanding access, not restricting it: a series of mortgagee letters reducing regulatory burden, the addition of VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T as acceptable credit scoring models, and continued support for down payment assistance stacking. The next annual report to Congress is due around November 2026, which is when these figures get refreshed.

Why should a Texas buyer pay closer attention than most?

Two reasons, and this is where it gets local.

First, Texas has shown up in the delinquency data. Texas was among the states with the largest quarterly increases in overall delinquencies in the reporting from Q3 2025.

Second, and more concretely: ICE reported that Texas and Florida together account for about 39% of underwater homes nationwide. That's the mechanism you should understand. A buyer who purchased in 2022–2024 with minimal money down, in a metro that had been appreciating fast, then met a market where prices went flat — that buyer has little or no equity. DFW prices have been running roughly flat to slightly down year over year through 2026. So a 3.5%-down FHA purchase today should be planned on the assumption that you will have very little equity for the first two or three years. Not zero appreciation forever — just no equity cushion in the near term.

That has one practical consequence: if something goes wrong in year two, you can't sell your way out, because the sale costs more than your equity. Your cushion has to be cash, not equity. Which brings us to the part that actually protects you.

What do I need to do to stay out of this statistic?

Five things, in order of how much they matter. The payment figures below use a $300,000 Dallas County home, FHA with 3.5% down, at Freddie Mac's current 7.40% benchmark and Dallas County's roughly 2.0% effective property tax rate:

Purchase price$300,000
Down payment (3.5%)$10,500
Loan amount (incl. 1.75% upfront MIP financed)$294,566
Principal & interest at 7.40%$2,039

6 months of reserves$17,082
  1. Build reserves anyway. FHA doesn't require them on a single-family purchase. Target three to six months of full PITI on top of your down payment and closing costs. I know that's a hard ask — even one or two months helps, and documented reserves are simultaneously one of the strongest compensating factors in underwriting. The money that protects you also helps you qualify.
  2. Plan for escrow shock. In Texas this is the single most common reason a payment jumps, and it catches people every year. If you buy a home where the seller had a homestead exemption and you don't yet, or you buy new construction where the land was taxed but not the house, your first-year escrow estimate will under-collect — and the re-analysis raises your payment. Here's exactly how that happens and what the increase looks like. File your homestead exemption as soon as you're eligible.
  3. Qualify under your maximum, not at it. FHA's automated system will approve some files past a 50% debt-to-income ratio. Being approved at 50% and buying at 50% are different decisions. If I tell you that you qualify for $340,000, buying at $300,000 is often the better call at a 7.40% rate.
  4. Don't add debt before or after closing. A new car loan is the number one file-killer I see, and the average new-car payment is now high enough to move a DTI by several points. After closing it's not a file risk — it's a cushion risk.
  5. Know your options before you need them. FHA has real loss mitigation tools — partial claims, forbearance, modifications — and the borrowers who come out fine are the ones who call in month one of trouble, not month four. Read the options now so you're not learning them under pressure.

A real example of how this plays out

I worked with a family in Mesquite a while back — FHA, 3.5% down on a $289,000 home, scores in the low 600s, combined income around $86,000. Their first-year payment escrowed at about $2,610 a month. The seller had held a homestead exemption for years, so the tax figure used to set their initial escrow was well below what the property would actually be assessed at under their ownership. At the first escrow re-analysis, their payment went to roughly $2,890 — a $280 jump they had not budgeted for, plus a shortage spread over twelve months.

That is exactly the sequence behind a chunk of that 11.79%. The difference in their case is that we had talked about it before closing, and they had about $9,000 set aside. They absorbed the shortage, filed their own homestead exemption, and their payment settled back down the following year. Nothing dramatic happened — which is the entire point. The escrow increase was predictable; the only variable was whether they had the cushion when it arrived.

How do I get started with FHA in DFW?

None of this is a reason to avoid FHA. For a lot of my clients it's the only program that works, and it's still the most accessible path to owning a home in Dallas, Garland, Mesquite or Fort Worth — 3.5% down at a 580 score, up to the DFW limit of $563,500 across every county in the metro, and it stacks with Texas down payment assistance.

What the delinquency data should change is your planning, not your program choice. Let's run your numbers, set a realistic purchase price rather than a maximum one, build an escrow estimate that reflects what your taxes will actually be instead of what the seller paid, and put a reserve target in writing. That conversation takes twenty minutes and it's the difference between the two sides of that statistic.
