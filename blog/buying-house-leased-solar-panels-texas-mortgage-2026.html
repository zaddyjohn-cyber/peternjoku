Buying a House With Leased Solar Panels in Texas: What Your Mortgage Lender Needs in 2026

Buying ProcessBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 20, 20268 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and solar panels have become one of the most common surprises in DFW resale deals — especially in the newer subdivisions around Royse City, Forney, Rockwall and McKinney. The buyer loves the house, the listing says "solar — low electric bills," and then two weeks into the contract the title company finds a UCC filing and the lender asks for a lease agreement nobody has seen. Getting ahead of that is the whole point of this post.

Two things changed recently that make this more relevant in late 2026. The federal 30% residential solar tax credit for homeowners who buy their own system (Section 25D) ended for systems installed after December 31, 2025, so more panels being sold now are leases and power purchase agreements (PPAs), where a solar company owns the equipment. And Texas's new solar sales law, SB 1036, gives buyers of new solar contracts a five-business-day right to cancel (in effect since September 1, 2025) and requires solar retailers and salespeople to register with TDLR starting September 1, 2026. Most of what ranks for this topic is written by agents for sellers. This one is for the buyer who needs a mortgage.

Can I get a mortgage on a house with leased solar panels in Texas?

Yes. Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA and VA all lend on homes with solar panels. What changes is paperwork, value and debt-to-income, and those depend entirely on who owns the panels. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide (section B2-3-04) sorts them into four buckets:

The most important row is the last one. Leased panels add nothing to the appraised value, so you can't pay extra for "the solar" and expect the appraisal to support it. If the price is above what comparable homes without solar sell for, that gap becomes a low appraisal problem.

Does a solar lease payment count against my debt-to-income ratio?

Under Fannie Mae's guideline, a monthly lease payment counts in your DTI — unless the lease guarantees a fixed amount of energy production and pays you back pro-rata if the panels underproduce. A PPA where you pay only for the electricity the panels actually produce can be left out of the ratio. So the exact wording of the agreement matters, and I need the full document, not the seller's summary.

Fannie also requires the lease or PPA to include certain protections before it will buy the loan:

  • The solar company is responsible for any damage from installation, malfunction or removal, and must repair it.
  • The solar company can't be named as a loss payee or insured on your homeowners policy.
  • If the home is ever foreclosed, the lender can terminate the agreement and have the panels removed, take over the agreement with no transfer fee, or sign a new one on terms no worse than yours.
  • The home has to keep access to a normal alternate power source — the grid.

And with a 2.9% escalator, the $142 becomes about $186 in ten years. The panels may lower your electric bill, but underwriters don't give you credit for lower utility bills. Budget both sides.

What is a UCC filing on solar panels and why does the title company care?

A UCC-1 filing is a public notice that a creditor has an interest in equipment — here, the panels. Solar lenders and leasing companies file them so they can recover the equipment if payments stop. Your lender needs its mortgage to be the first lien on the home, so the title company has to deal with any solar filing before closing. Depending on the type:

  • Solar loan: usually paid off from the seller's proceeds at closing, with the filing terminated afterward.
  • Lease or PPA: the filing is often "precautionary" — it says the solar company owns the panels, not that it has a lien on your house. The title company may let it stay if it's clearly limited to the equipment, but it decides that, not the agent.

Ask for a UCC search early. In Texas, these are searchable through the Secretary of State, and your title company will run one. More on title costs and what they cover in my Texas title insurance guide.

How do I protect myself when making an offer on a house with solar?

  1. Ask before you offer: Owned, financed, leased or PPA? Who is the company? Monthly payment and escalator? Years left? Payoff or buyout amount?
  2. Get the full agreement and send it to me during your option period — not after. I'll tell you whether it counts in your DTI and whether it meets agency requirements.
  3. Use TREC's fixture lease addendum. Texas has a promulgated addendum for leased fixtures like solar panels. It gives you a window to review the lease and terminate if you don't approve it.
  4. Start the transfer application immediately. Solar companies run their own credit check and approval, and it can take several weeks. Ask the company for its current timeline.
  5. Price it without the panels. Since leased panels don't add appraised value, base your offer on comparable homes without solar.
  6. Consider a buyout. Sometimes the cleanest fix is asking the seller to buy out the lease or pay off the loan from their proceeds.

Can an HOA in Texas make me remove solar panels?

Generally not. Texas Property Code Section 202.010 limits an HOA's power to ban solar energy devices, with some exceptions, such as placement and certain HOA-controlled common areas. For a buyer, the bigger HOA issue is usually dues, which count in your DTI just like the lease payment does.

A Royse City buyer whose deal was saved by reading the lease

A couple I worked with this year — both teachers, credit scores in the high 600s — went under contract on a Royse City home with leased panels. The seller's listing said the system "saves $150 a month." The lease said $139 a month with a 2.9% annual escalator and 17 years left, and it had no production guarantee, so the full payment counted in their DTI. With it, their ratio landed at 47%, just over what their conventional approval would allow.

We had three options: walk away during the option period, ask the seller to buy out the lease, or switch to an FHA loan with a higher DTI allowance. Their agent negotiated a seller-paid lease buyout as part of the deal, the title company cleared the filing, and they closed with no solar payment at all — and panels that were now theirs. If we'd found the lease after the option period, they'd have lost that leverage.
