Builder Preferred Lender vs Outside Lender in DFW Texas 2026: Rate Math, Incentives, and When Each One Wins

New ConstructionBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 15, 20268 min read

You found a new-construction home in DFW, you love it, and the builder's sales agent just handed you a flyer with a 5.0% interest rate. Meanwhile, you know the current market rate is around 7.40%. That's a huge difference — $545/month on a $350,000 loan. So you're wondering: is the builder's preferred lender actually offering me a deal, or is there a catch?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a licensed Mortgage Loan Officer (NMLS #2670329) based in Garland. I'm an outside lender. That means I'm not going to pretend builder lenders are always a bad deal — sometimes they're genuinely the better choice. But I've reviewed enough builder incentive packages to know when the math works and when the marketing is doing more work than the numbers. Here's what I tell buyers in DFW every week.

The October 2026 rate landscape in DFW

The Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey (PMMS) 30-year fixed rate was 7.40% as of the week of October 8, 2026 — the seventh consecutive week of rate increases and the highest level since November 2023. This is the baseline outside-lender rate. Builder incentive programs typically offer 200–250 basis points below market through rate buydown subsidies funded by the builder's margin.

Major builders in DFW — D.R. Horton, Lennar, Pulte, Highland Homes, Meritage — are currently offering incentive rates in the 4.75%–5.25% range. The exact rate depends on which community, which home type, how long the home has been sitting on market, and whether you're buying a spec (move-in-ready) home vs. a to-be-built.

How builder-subsidized rates work: where does the money come from?

Builders fund the rate subsidy one of two ways — and knowing which one matters:

  1. A genuine permanent buydown: The builder pays discount points on your behalf to your lender (in this case, their preferred lender) to buy your rate down permanently. This is real money the builder is spending. The cost to buy a rate from 7.40% down to 5.0% is roughly 6–8 discount points, which on $350,000 = $21,000–$28,000. That's the builder's subsidy. This is legitimate if the builder isn't inflating the home price to recoup it.
  2. An inflated purchase price that funds the buydown: The builder lists the home at $395,000 instead of $365,000. The extra $30,000 is used to pay the points. You're "getting" a low rate but paying for it in a higher purchase price — and a higher starting loan balance. You've also paid more than market value for the home.

The way to check: ask an outside lender what the home would appraise for at $395,000. If the comparable sales support $395,000, the price may be fair. If the appraised value comes in below the purchase price, the builder has priced in the subsidy and you're not getting a free lunch.

When outside financing wins

There are three situations where I tell buyers to look hard at outside financing even when the builder is offering a below-market rate:

  1. You're a veteran eligible for a VA loan. VA loans have no monthly mortgage insurance premium (MIP/PMI), require $0 down, and the VA funding fee can be financed. On a $350,000 purchase with a VA loan at 7.40%, you're putting $0 down. With the builder at 5.0%, you may still need a down payment to get that incentive rate — and without the VA benefit. I've seen cases where a VA loan at market rate saves more over five years than the builder's subsidized conventional loan.
  2. You qualify for stacked DPA programs. TSAHC, TDHCA, Dallas DHAP, and Fort Worth HAP programs must be used through their approved lender network — not builder in-house lenders. If you qualify for $20,000 in down payment assistance through Dallas DHAP (forgivable over 5 years), that's real money that only flows through outside lenders. A 7.40% rate with $20,000 in free down payment assistance may beat a 5.0% builder rate with $5,000 in closing credit.
  3. You plan to refinance within 2–3 years. If you believe market rates will fall significantly in the next 2–3 years and you'll refinance, the builder's rate advantage is shorter-lived than the 5-year math suggests. The break-even timeline may extend past your refinance horizon, and you've locked into the builder lender unnecessarily.

When builder financing wins

Builder financing is the right call when:

  • The rate subsidy is genuine (not hidden in an inflated purchase price), the buydown is permanent (not a 3-2-1 temporary buydown that expires), and you plan to stay in the home for 5+ years
  • You don't qualify for DPA programs or VA, and you need the lower payment to qualify at all
  • The builder is offering a spec home that needs to close within 45–60 days, and the builder lender's familiarity with the community speeds up the process
  • The combined incentive package (rate + closing cost credits + appliances + upgrades) adds up to a dollar value that meaningfully exceeds what an outside lender can offer

A real comparison: Forney buyer choosing between DHI Mortgage and me

A couple I worked with in October 2026 was buying a $372,000 D.R. Horton home in Forney. DHI Mortgage offered them: 5.0% rate, $8,000 in closing cost credit, and a refrigerator/washer/dryer package valued at approximately $3,500. Total incentive value: ~$11,500 in credits plus the rate savings.

My outside quote: 7.40% rate, $5,000 in lender credits, and I could stack them with a TSAHC grant (they qualified) worth $13,600 (3.5% of the $388,000 loan amount after accounting for the seller contribution). They also had 620 credit scores — qualifying for the FHA TSAHC combination.

Monthly payment comparison:

  • DHI Mortgage at 5.0%: $1,879/month on $350K conventional = $1,997/month on the full $372K
  • My FHA loan at 7.40%: $2,580/month on $372K FHA + $112 MIP = $2,692/month

Monthly difference: $695/month lower with the builder. Over 5 years: $41,700. The TSAHC grant saved them $13,600 upfront in cash, but the monthly payment was $695 higher. The break-even on the TSAHC grant's value vs. the monthly savings was roughly 20 months ($13,600 ÷ $695). After 20 months, the DHI rate was saving them more per month than the TSAHC grant had been worth.

They chose DHI Mortgage, and it was the right call for their situation. I tell that story because it's not always an easy answer — and because I'd rather help you make the right choice than make the pitch for the obvious one.

Can the builder legally require me to use their preferred lender?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329) — no. Under RESPA, a builder cannot make your purchase contingent on using their preferred lender. They can make their incentive package contingent on it — which is legal — but they can't refuse to sell you the home if you choose outside financing. Your leverage: negotiate directly with the builder for cash credits or a lower purchase price if you're going outside, especially on a spec home where they want to close fast.
