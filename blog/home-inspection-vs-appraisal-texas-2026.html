Home Inspection vs Appraisal in Texas: Who Orders What, and What Your Lender Actually Sees

Buying ProcessBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 27, 20268 min read

I get this question in some form almost every week, usually phrased as "wait, I thought the appraisal already checked the house." It didn't. In a Texas purchase you are paying for two completely different documents, ordered by two different people, for two different purposes, on two different clocks — and only one of them ever reaches your underwriter.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland (NMLS #2670329). The pages that rank for this question are national explainers from the big portals. They define the two words side by side and stop, which is fine as far as it goes, but it leaves out everything that actually decides your closing: which document my underwriter reads, when each one gives you leverage, and the specific situation where an appraiser starts behaving like an inspector. Let me fill that in with the Texas detail.

What's the difference between a home inspection and an appraisal in Texas?

Said plainly: the inspection tells you what you are buying. The appraisal tells my lender what it is worth. An inspector's job is condition. An appraiser's job is value. A perfectly appraised house can have a failing HVAC and a cracked drain line, and the appraisal will not mention either one.

Does my underwriter actually see my inspection report?

No. This is the single most useful thing in this article, and no page ranking for this keyword says it.

Your inspection report is a document you commissioned for your own decision-making. It does not get submitted with the loan. I have had buyers hand me a 60-page report expecting underwriting to weigh in, and that is not how the file works — my underwriter reviews the appraisal, the title commitment, your income and credit documents, and the contract. Not your inspector's findings.

Which has a consequence buyers never anticipate: if your inspection turns up something serious and you decide to buy anyway, nothing in the loan process will stop you. The lender is not a second opinion on condition. The decision is yours, and the option period is where you make it.

When does an appraiser start acting like an inspector?

Here is the exception, and in DFW it comes up constantly because so much of our first-time-buyer inventory is FHA.

On FHA and VA loans, the appraiser is doing two jobs at once: estimating value and checking the property against minimum standards built on safety, security and soundness. So the appraiser becomes the one person whose condition findings land directly in my loan file. Things I see called out:

  • Peeling or chipping paint on a home built before 1978 (a lead-based paint concern)
  • Exposed wiring, missing outlet covers, an open electrical panel
  • Non-functioning HVAC, or no permanent heat source
  • An active roof leak or a roof at the end of its serviceable life
  • Missing handrails, broken steps, a trip hazard at the entry
  • Standing water, major drainage against the foundation, evidence of structural movement
  • An empty, green or unfenced swimming pool

When an appraiser writes one of those up, it becomes a repair condition that has to be cleared before closing, usually followed by a re-inspection with its own fee. A conventional appraisal generally does not work this way — the appraiser notes condition as it affects value and moves on, unless something is severe enough to make the property unmarketable. That difference is why the same house can sail through on conventional financing and generate a repair list on FHA. I wrote the detail up in FHA minimum property requirements in Texas.

That FHA case number detail matters more than it sounds: switching lenders on the same case number keeps the existing value, while a new case number generally means a new appraisal and a new fee.

Where do the inspection and the appraisal sit in my Texas contract?

Two separate clocks, and mixing them up is how buyers lose leverage.

The inspection lives in the option period. You pay an option fee for a set number of days during which you can terminate for any reason and get your earnest money back. That is the only window where an inspection finding is genuine leverage — ask for a repair, ask for a credit, or walk. Once the option period expires, your inspection report is just information. My guide to earnest money and the option fee covers the mechanics.

The appraisal lives under the Third Party Financing Addendum. The TPFA splits into Buyer Approval, which is about you qualifying, and Property Approval, which is about the property supporting the loan — including the appraised value. The Property Approval right runs much later than people expect: you can terminate under it up to the third day before the closing date. That is your protection if the appraisal comes in low, and it is why I never tell a buyer to waive the addendum. More in my TPFA guide.

So the practical order looks like this:

  • Days 1–3: you order the general inspection and any add-ons. Insurance quotes go out the same week.
  • Days 3–7: report back. Negotiate repairs or credit, or terminate. This is your leverage window.
  • After Buyer Approval moves: I order the appraisal. Expect roughly a week to ten days for the report in a normal DFW market, longer on rural or unusual properties.
  • If the appraiser writes up repairs: they get completed, then a re-inspection confirms it, with its own fee and a few more days.
  • Third day before closing: the outside edge of your Property Approval termination right.

Do I qualify, and what does this look like with real numbers?

Last year I worked with a first-time buyer in Mesquite on a $285,000 1970s brick home, FHA, 3.5% down, credit in the low 600s. Two separate things happened that she had assumed were one thing.

Her inspector found a water heater past its service life and a sub-panel with double-tapped breakers. Neither of those reached my underwriter — but because she found them on day four, the seller replaced the water heater and credited her $1,200 toward the electrical work. That was option-period leverage doing its job.

Then the FHA appraiser, three weeks later, called out peeling paint on the detached garage's pre-1978 trim. That one did land in my file as a repair condition. The seller scraped and repainted, we paid a re-inspection fee, and we closed eight days later than the original date.

Her loan amount came to about $275,000. At 7.32% — the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey 30-year average for the week of October 22, 2026, down from 7.40% the prior week and 6.19% a year earlier — principal and interest was about $1,889 a month. With Dallas County taxes near 2.0% ($475/month), insurance around $200, and FHA mortgage insurance, her payment landed close to $2,760. Total out of pocket for both reports plus the re-inspection was under $900 — on a deal where the inspection alone recovered roughly $2,500 of value.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my mortgage lender see my home inspection report in Texas?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and no — as a rule your inspection report does not go into the loan file. You hire the inspector, you own the report, and it exists to inform your decision and your negotiation with the seller. The appraisal is the opposite: my lender orders it, and it's the document underwriting actually reviews. The one place they overlap is FHA and VA, where the appraiser also checks minimum property standards and can turn a safety item into a repair condition. So an inspection finding doesn't reach my underwriter, but the same defect can reach them through the appraiser. Call or text me at 469-545-7180.

Who pays for the appraisal and the inspection in a Texas home purchase?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and the buyer pays for both — but differently. You arrange and pay the inspector directly, typically $300 to $500, with add-ons like termite, sewer scope, mold or pool running another $100 to $300 each. The appraisal is ordered by the lender and paid by you either when it's ordered or through your closing costs; a Texas guide citing Angi puts 2026 appraisal fees around $314 to $424, though in DFW I see more than that on larger or rural properties. You choose your inspector. You do not choose the appraiser. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 for a cost sheet on your purchase.

Can I skip the home inspection to make my Texas offer stronger?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and I'd push back hard on that in the fall 2026 DFW market. In 2021 waiving an inspection was how you won a house. Today, with inventory up and sellers making concessions on about half of DFW closings, you don't need to give away your only look under the hood. Your option period is what you paid the option fee for — it's the one window where a finding is real leverage. Skipping the appraisal is a different question and usually isn't yours to make, since the lender requires it. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and I'll tell you what your offer actually needs.

What happens if the appraisal comes in low on my Texas home purchase?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and the loan gets capped at the appraised value, not your contract price — so the gap has to come from somewhere. You have four realistic paths: renegotiate the price with the seller, cover the difference in cash, split it, or terminate under the Third Party Financing Addendum's Property Approval provision and get your earnest money back. In this market I'm renegotiating far more often than I'm asking buyers for cash. The Property Approval right runs to the third day before closing, which is more time than most buyers realize.
