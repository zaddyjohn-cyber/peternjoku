Mortgage Rates Fall to 7.18% for the Third Consecutive Week: What DFW Texas Buyers Should Do Now

Mortgage RatesBy Bond Peter NjokuNovember 6, 20267 min read

Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey (PMMS) released November 5, 2026 showed the 30-year fixed mortgage rate falling to 7.18% — the third consecutive weekly decline from a year-high 7.40% peak reached in early October. The 15-year fixed averaged 6.57%, and a year ago, rates were at 6.22%.

I'm watching this closely because the trajectory matters as much as the number. If you are a DFW buyer who was waiting on the sidelines because rates felt too high, the question now is: has the window opened enough to act, and is this trend likely to continue?

My short answer: yes to the first question, uncertain to the second — and I'll show you the math behind both.

What does the Freddie Mac PMMS 7.18% rate actually mean for a DFW buyer's payment?

The PMMS is a national survey average, not a rate quote. Your actual rate depends on your credit score, loan type (FHA vs. conventional), loan amount, and down payment. But the PMMS tells us the benchmark — and right now that benchmark has moved meaningfully.

That is real money, but it is also not the difference between affording and not affording a home. The bigger picture is that every drop makes qualifying easier: lower payment = lower required income at a given DTI.

What drove the three-week rate decline from 7.40% to 7.18%?

Mortgage rates move with the 10-year Treasury yield. The October 7.40% peak reflected markets pricing in persistent inflation — the 10-year briefly touched 5.19% in mid-October before easing to around 5.08–5.11% by the end of the month. That easing pulled PMMS with it.

The midterm elections on November 4, 2026 appear to have added some stability — bond markets historically respond positively to political clarity, whatever the outcome. The midterm results are being parsed for fiscal policy implications, and an unchanged or gridlocked Congress typically reduces the risk of large new deficit spending, which is modestly positive for bonds and therefore for rates.

Why should DFW buyers consider locking now instead of waiting for rates to fall further?

Three reasons I would lean toward locking now rather than floating.

First, the December FOMC meeting carries real hike risk. The October 28 FOMC meeting kept rates at 3.75%–4.00%, but two of the ten voting members — Governors Cook and Hammack — dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike. That is unusual, and it signals that the committee is not uniformly dovish. If the November jobs report or CPI surprises to the upside, a December hike becomes more likely, which would push mortgage rates back up quickly.

Second, rate locks are free and usually 30–45 days. Locking at 7.18% costs you nothing. If rates drop further before closing, some lenders offer one-time float-down options. If rates rise, you are protected. There is genuine asymmetry in your favor when you lock in a declining environment.

Third, buying power improvement at 7.18% vs. 7.40% is already meaningful. At 7.40%, a household qualifying at the standard 43% back-end DTI with $7,500/month gross income can support roughly $3,225/month in total debt payments. At 7.18%, the same household gets approximately $3,225/month but the lower P&I means they can qualify for a slightly larger loan — the difference is around $5,000–$8,000 in purchase price at these rate levels.

Last October I helped a first-time buyer in Garland who had been watching rates since summer 2026. At 7.40%, the payment on the $312,000 home he found was about $2,113 per month in principal and interest — right at the top of his qualifying range. When rates came down to 7.18% the following week, the same purchase price dropped to $2,075 per month, giving him a $38 monthly cushion that made his DTI work. He locked, we closed, and he owns the home now. The buyers still waiting for 6% rates are still waiting.

What does the 15-year fixed rate at 6.57% mean for refinancers?

If you bought a home in 2024 or early 2025 when rates were in the 6.5–7.0% range, a 15-year at 6.57% might be nearly identical to your existing 30-year rate — but the payment is higher because of the shorter term. The refinance math rarely works in that scenario.

Where the 15-year makes sense: if you have significant equity, want to accelerate payoff, and can comfortably handle the higher payment. On a $300,000 balance, the 30-year at 7.18% is $2,032/month P&I; the 15-year at 6.57% is $2,625/month P&I. That $593 premium buys you 15 fewer years and roughly $190,000 in total interest savings over the life of the loan.

Call me and I will run a side-by-side comparison for your specific loan balance.

Should DFW buyers act before the December 11 USDA shutdown deadline?

USDA-eligible buyers in Forney, Royse City, and Terrell have an additional urgency layer: the federal government is funded only through December 11, 2026, and a lapse would freeze new USDA Loan Note Guarantee issuance. If you have a USDA loan under contract, you want your guarantee issued well before December 11. Standard USDA processing in Texas currently runs 3–5 business days after lender submission, but that can extend during high-volume periods. With six weeks to that deadline, the window is tighter than it looks. Text me at 469-545-7180 if you are in a USDA-eligible area and I will walk you through the timeline.
