ARM Mortgage Reset Wave 2026–2027: What Texas Homeowners Must Do Now

Rate UpdateBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 13, 20268 min read

If you took out an adjustable-rate mortgage between 2019 and 2022, your loan may be approaching its first rate reset — and in today's rate environment, that means a payment increase that could catch you off guard. I'm tracking this closely because it's directly affecting DFW homeowners I work with, and the numbers coming out this fall are significant.

According to ICE Mortgage Technology data published in October 2026, approximately 148,000 adjustable-rate mortgages will reset in 2026 — 84,000 already completed through July, with another 64,000 scheduled between August and December. The reset wave grows in 2027, with 186,020 ARMs projected to adjust. If your loan is in that pipeline, you have options — but you need to understand them now, before the notice arrives.

How much will an ARM payment increase after a reset?

It depends heavily on which year you originated your loan and what initial rate you received. ICE's analysis breaks it down by cohort:

ARM CohortOriginal Rate (approx.)Likely Reset Rate (Oct 2026)Median Monthly Increase
7-year ARMs from 2020~2.5–3.5%~7.80–8.30% (SOFR + margin, subject to caps)+$1,066/month (+36%)
5-year ARMs from 2022~4.0–5.0%~7.80–8.30% (SOFR + margin, subject to caps)+$430/month (+22%)
ARMs originated 2023–present~6.0–7.0%~7.80–8.30% at first reset (2027–2028)Modest increase or near-flat with caps

These numbers assume caps limit the jump. Most conventional ARMs follow a 5/2/5 cap structure: a maximum 5% increase at the first reset, 2% at each subsequent annual adjustment, and a 5% lifetime cap above the initial rate. So if your loan started at 3.0%, your rate can't jump past 8.0% on the first reset — but it can still land there.

What is SOFR and how does it determine my new rate?

SOFR stands for Secured Overnight Financing Rate, published daily by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. It replaced LIBOR as the standard adjustable-rate index for most U.S. mortgages starting in 2023. When your ARM resets, your lender calculates:

New rate = SOFR index + your loan's margin

As of October 2026, SOFR is approximately 5.30% — reflecting the Federal Reserve's target rate range of 3.75–4.00% and market dynamics. The typical conventional ARM margin is 2.50–3.00%, which means the fully indexed rate today is approximately 7.80–8.30%. Your specific margin is written into your loan note — dig it out of your closing documents or call your servicer to get that number.

Your cap structure is equally important. If your initial rate was 3.0% and your first-adjustment cap is 5%, your rate cannot exceed 8.0% at the first reset — even though the fully indexed rate is higher. That cap provides some protection, but 8.0% is still meaningfully higher than 3.0%, and the payment impact is real.

A real DFW scenario: When a refinance makes sense even at 7.40%

A homeowner in Plano recently reached out to me — they took a 7/1 ARM in October 2019 at 3.125% on a $420,000 loan. Their fixed period ended October 2026. With SOFR at 5.30% and a 2.75% margin, their fully indexed rate would be 8.05%. Their initial cap allows a 5% jump maximum from 3.125%, so the first-year reset cap limits them to 8.125% — but that's essentially the full indexed rate.

At 8.125% on a remaining balance of approximately $375,000 (after 7 years of payments), the new payment would be about $2,801/month — up from their original $1,807. That's nearly $1,000 more per month.

When I showed them they could refinance to a 30-year fixed at 7.40%, their new payment would be approximately $2,582/month. That's $219/month less than the reset rate — and it eliminates all future adjustment risk. Yes, 7.40% is a historically elevated rate. But compared to their alternative of 8.125%, it was the right move for their situation.

How to find out when your ARM resets and what your new payment will be

If your ARM is approaching its first reset, federal law (TILA's ARM adjustment notice rules) requires your servicer to send you a payment estimate 7–8 months before the first payment at the new rate. For subsequent resets, the notice must arrive 2–4 months before the adjustment. If you haven't received a notice and believe your reset is near, call your servicer directly and ask for:

  • Your loan's index (should be SOFR for modern ARMs)
  • Your margin (typically 2.50–3.00%)
  • Your cap structure (first adjustment cap, periodic cap, lifetime cap)
  • The projected new rate and payment based on the current index

Once you have those numbers, you can run a comparison against refinancing into a fixed rate. I'm happy to do that analysis for you — just call or text me at 469-545-7180 and give me those four numbers, and I'll tell you within 15 minutes whether refinancing makes mathematical sense right now.

Can I use a VA IRRRL or FHA Streamline to refinance out of an ARM?

Yes — and these are often faster and cheaper than a conventional refinance. The VA Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan (IRRRL) allows veterans to refinance from an ARM to a fixed-rate VA loan with minimal documentation and no new appraisal required in most cases. The only requirement is that you must be getting a lower rate or moving from an ARM to a fixed rate. If your current ARM rate is resetting higher than your potential fixed rate, you qualify.

Similarly, the FHA Streamline refinance lets FHA ARM borrowers move to a fixed-rate FHA loan with reduced documentation — typically no income verification or new appraisal. There are net tangible benefit rules (you must demonstrate a meaningful benefit), but moving from an ARM to a fixed rate generally satisfies that automatically.

If your original ARM was a conventional loan (Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac), you're looking at a standard rate-and-term refinance — which still works but requires full underwriting. Call me at 469-545-7180 to determine which refinance path applies to your loan type.
