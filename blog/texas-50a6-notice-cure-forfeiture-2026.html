Texas 50(a)(6) Notice-Cure-Forfeiture Rule: What Every Cash-Out Refi Homeowner Should Know

RefinanceBy Bond Peter NjokuNovember 6, 20269 min read

Texas has one of the strongest homestead protection frameworks in the United States, and nowhere is that more visible than in Article XVI, Section 50(a)(6) of the Texas Constitution — the provision governing cash-out (homestead equity) refinances. If a lender violates any of the 12 specific requirements imposed by that provision, Texas law gives you a path to hold them accountable that no other state offers: a formal notice-and-cure process that, if the lender fails to respond properly, results in forfeiture of all principal and all interest on the loan.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a Garland-based mortgage loan officer. I work through 50(a)(6) loans regularly, and I want to explain this rule in plain language — what it covers, what it protects, and what happens when it is violated.

What is a Texas 50(a)(6) loan?

A Texas 50(a)(6) loan is a homestead equity loan — a refinance of your primary residence in which you receive cash from your home's equity. In Texas, you cannot take cash out of your homestead through any other loan structure. Second mortgages, HELOCs secured by your homestead, and any refinance that results in you receiving cash are all 50(a)(6) loans, subject to the constitutional requirements.

What is NOT a 50(a)(6) loan: a rate-and-term refinance of an existing 50(a)(6) loan that does not produce additional cash is called a 50(f)(2) refinance. This type of loan is not subject to the strict 50(a)(6) requirements and can close on a standard timeline without the special disclosures or waiting periods. Many Texas homeowners who refinanced for a better rate and wonder why the process felt different from what they expected are probably doing a 50(f)(2), not a new 50(a)(6).

How does the notice-and-cure process work?

The notice-and-cure right comes from §50(a)(6)(Q)(x). Here is how it works step by step:

  1. Owner gives written notice. If you believe your lender violated one or more of the 12 requirements, you send the lender written notice specifically identifying which requirement was violated and how.
  2. Lender has 60 calendar days to cure. From the date the lender receives your written notice, they have 60 calendar days to cure the violation. The Texas Constitution specifies six cure methods, including: adjusting the loan terms, paying you a refund equal to any fees collected in excess of 2%, returning all fees collected in a closing-location violation, or refinancing the loan into a compliant form.
  3. Failure to cure = forfeiture. If the lender fails to cure within 60 days, you are entitled to file suit for a court order declaring the lender has forfeited its right to collect all principal and all interest on the loan. In practice, this means the entire loan balance is extinguished — the lender gets nothing.
  4. No cure obligation if lender disputes the violation. The lender can dispute your notice — if they believe no violation occurred, they do not have to cure. That dispute then goes to litigation. This is why consulting a Texas real estate attorney before sending notice is important.

What are the most common violations in practice?

The 12-day waiting period (Requirements 4 and 5). These are the most common violations I see. The 12 calendar days must run from the later of (a) when the lender gives the required disclosures, or (b) when the owner submits the loan application. If a borrower submits on a Monday and the lender tries to close the following Friday — 11 days later — that is a violation. Many lenders miscalculate because they count from the application date but forget that the disclosure date may be later, or vice versa.

Closing location (Requirement 6). The loan must close at a permanent office — a lender's branch, an attorney's law office, or a title company's office. It cannot close at the borrower's home, even if the borrower requests it. Remote online notarization (RON) closings for 50(a)(6) loans require specific structuring to meet this requirement. Lenders who send a notary to the borrower's kitchen table for a cash-out refinance are almost certainly in violation.

The 2% fee cap (Requirement 7). Total fees — origination charges, underwriting fees, processing fees, points — cannot exceed 2% of the loan amount. On a $300,000 cash-out refinance, that cap is $6,000. This calculation excludes certain title insurance, tax-related fees, and appraisal costs (which are excluded from the 2% cap), but many lenders miscalculate by including or excluding the wrong items. If you paid more than 2% in lender fees on a Texas cash-out refi, that is a potential violation.

Can a Texas 50(a)(6) loan get an appraisal waiver?

No. This is one of the most important distinctions between Texas 50(a)(6) loans and conventional rate-and-term refinances. Under Fannie Mae guidelines (B7-2-05 and B5-4.1-01), Texas homestead equity loans require a full appraisal — no appraisal waivers are permitted, regardless of what the Desktop Underwriter (DU) automated system returns. Even if DU comes back with an appraisal waiver offer, that waiver cannot be used for a 50(a)(6) loan. Budget time (typically 7–14 days for scheduling and delivery) and cost ($500–$750 in DFW) for the appraisal.

By contrast, a 50(f)(2) rate-and-term refinance of a prior 50(a)(6) loan may qualify for an appraisal waiver under standard Fannie/Freddie guidelines, depending on LTV and loan history.

How does the 50(a)(6) forfeiture risk affect lenders?

Texas 50(a)(6) lender liability is not theoretical. Courts have upheld the forfeiture remedy in cases involving fee cap violations, closing location violations, and waiting period violations. The asymmetry is severe: a relatively minor procedural violation — say, a lender closing three days too early — can result in the lender forfeiting a $300,000 loan balance.

This is why reputable Texas lenders are extremely careful with 50(a)(6) loans. I am careful with every cash-out refi I originate in Texas — the compliance requirements are real, the timelines matter, and mistakes cannot be undone after closing. If you are considering a Texas cash-out refinance and your current lender seems to be rushing or cutting corners on the process, that is a warning sign.

What is the current environment for Texas cash-out refinances in November 2026?

With the 30-year fixed rate at 7.18% (Freddie Mac PMMS, November 5, 2026) — still elevated compared to the 2021–2022 era — most of the Texas refinance market is cash-out rather than rate-and-term. Homeowners who locked in at 3% or 4% will not refinance just to lower their rate; but homeowners who have accumulated substantial equity and need funds for home improvement, college, or debt consolidation may still find cash-out attractive even at 7.18%.

The LTV cap still applies: 80% of appraised value. On a $400,000 appraised home, the maximum loan is $320,000. If your current balance is $280,000, the maximum cash out is $40,000 — before any fees and payoffs. The 2% fee cap on a $320,000 loan is $6,400 in lender fees, so a 50(a)(6) refi at higher rates tends to make sense only if you need a meaningful sum and can tolerate the higher payment.
