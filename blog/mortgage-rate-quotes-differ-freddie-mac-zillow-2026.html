Why Is My Mortgage Rate Quote Different From Freddie Mac, Zillow and the News? (October 2026)

RatesBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 19, 20266 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and last week a buyer in Rockwall sent me a screenshot from Zillow showing 7.14%, a news headline saying rates had "blown past 7.5%," and the Freddie Mac number of 7.40% — all from the same week — and asked, reasonably, "Which one is real?"

All of them. None of them is your rate. Each tracker measures something different, on a different day, for a different imaginary borrower. Here's what each one actually measures, what the spread costs in dollars on a DFW loan, and the eight things that set the rate on your quote.

What did each mortgage rate tracker say in October 2026?

SourceReadingDateWhat it measures
Freddie Mac PMMS7.40% (15-yr 6.71%)Week of Oct 15, 2026Weekly average from mortgage applications, for borrowers with excellent credit, 20% down, one-unit owner-occupied
Mortgage News Daily7.56%Oct 14, 2026Daily index from lender rate sheets for a top-tier borrower
Bankrate7.53%Week of Oct 7, 2026Survey of lenders' offered rates
MBA weekly survey7.50% (contract rate)Week ended Oct 9, 2026Contract rates on applications received, with points reported separately
Zillow7.14%Oct 17, 2026Quotes posted by lenders in Zillow's own marketplace

I use Freddie Mac as the benchmark because it's the longest-running series and the one most lenders and news outlets reference. I never average these together — they aren't measuring the same thing, so the average doesn't mean anything.

Why do Freddie Mac, Mortgage News Daily and Zillow show different rates?

  • Timing. Freddie Mac publishes once a week, on Thursday. Mortgage News Daily updates every day. When rates move fast — like when the 10-year Treasury jumped after the 3.7% September inflation report — the daily number runs ahead of the weekly one.
  • The borrower. Each source assumes a near-perfect borrower, but the details differ (credit score cutoffs, 20% vs 25% down).
  • Points. Some headline rates include discount points the borrower pays upfront. A rate with one point paid is not comparable to a rate with zero points.
  • Source of the data. Applications, lender rate sheets, lender surveys and marketplace ads all capture different slices of the market. Advertised rates on a marketplace skew to the most aggressive offers.

Here's a 30-year, $350,000 loan — about a $370,000–$390,000 DFW home with 5–10% down — at each reading:

What sets the rate on my own mortgage quote?

  1. Credit score tier. Conventional loans carry loan-level price adjustments by score band; a 680 prices noticeably worse than a 760.
  2. Down payment / loan-to-value. Less down usually means higher pricing on conventional loans.
  3. Loan type. FHA and VA often price lower than conventional for the same borrower, but carry their own insurance or funding fee.
  4. Points. Paying discount points buys the rate down; a lender credit raises it.
  5. Occupancy. Second homes and investment properties price higher than a primary residence.
  6. Cash-out. Texas 50(a)(6) cash-out refinances price above purchases and rate-and-term refinances.
  7. Lock period. A 60-day lock costs more than a 30-day lock.
  8. The day you price. Lenders reprice when the bond market moves — sometimes more than once a day.

How do I compare mortgage quotes from different lenders fairly?

Get Loan Estimates on the same day, for the same loan amount, loan type and lock period, and compare Section A (origination charges) on page 2 — that's where points hide. A 7.125% quote with $7,000 in points is not better than a 7.375% quote with none unless you'll keep the loan long enough to recover the cost. On a $350,000 loan, $7,000 to save about $60 a month takes roughly ten years to break even — longer than most buyers keep a loan when they expect to refinance.

A Rockwall buyer who chased the lowest headline

The Rockwall buyer with the Zillow screenshot had a 702 credit score, 5% down, and was looking at a $360,000 home. The 7.14% he'd seen assumed a borrower with much higher credit and more down, plus a point paid upfront. His real conventional quote came in at 7.625% with no points. We priced an FHA loan instead — his score lands better on FHA's pricing — and he locked at 7.25% with no points, a payment within $20 of what he'd been hoping for. The lowest headline wasn't his rate, but understanding why got him to a better one. If you're shopping in Rockwall, see my Rockwall mortgage page and how to get pre-approved in Rockwall.

Should I wait for mortgage rates to come down?

The forecasts just moved the wrong way. In October 2026 Fannie Mae raised its outlook to about 7.4% for the fourth quarter of 2026 and a 7.5% average for 2027, up from 6.7% a month earlier, after September CPI came in at 3.7%. The MBA is a little more optimistic at roughly 7.1%–7.2% through 2027. Forecasts are guesses and they change, but I wouldn't plan a purchase around the headline number falling a full point.
