Why 11% of DFW Buyers Are Choosing Adjustable-Rate Mortgages in October 2026 — And Whether You Should Too

Loan StrategyBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 13, 20268 min read

With the 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 7.40% — the highest it's been since November 2023 — a growing number of DFW homebuyers are turning to adjustable-rate mortgages to reduce their initial payment. ICE Mortgage Technology and the Mortgage Bankers Association reported that ARM applications hit approximately 11% of all rate locks in early October 2026, the highest share since late 2022. The MBA's weekly survey for the week ending October 2 showed ARMs at 10.3% of total applications, with MBA economist Joel Kan noting that borrowers are increasingly opting for ARMs to lower initial payments.

This is a real trend, and it's rational given the math. But not every DFW buyer should follow it — the answer depends on your timeline, budget, and risk tolerance. Here's the honest breakdown.

What is the actual rate difference between an ARM and a 30-year fixed right now?

In September and October 2026, MBA's data showed ARM rates averaging more than 1 percentage point below the 30-year fixed rate. In practice, 5/1 ARM rates from Texas lenders are being quoted in the 6.00–6.50% range, compared to 7.40% for the 30-year fixed (Freddie Mac PMMS, week of October 8, 2026).

Using 6.25% as a representative 5/1 ARM rate, here's what the payment difference looks like for a $350,000 DFW loan:

What is the worst-case scenario after my ARM resets?

Understanding the cap structure of your specific ARM is non-negotiable before signing. Most conventional ARMs in 2026 carry a 5/2/5 cap structure:

  • Initial cap: 5% — the rate cannot increase more than 5 percentage points above the start rate at the first adjustment
  • Periodic cap: 2% — each subsequent annual adjustment cannot exceed 2 percentage points
  • Lifetime cap: 5% — the rate can never exceed the start rate plus 5 percentage points over the life of the loan

Your initial cap protects you from jumping straight to 11.25%, but an increase from $2,155 to $2,472 is still +$317/month — meaningful, though not catastrophic for most DFW buyers who budgeted carefully.

Who should seriously consider an ARM in October 2026?

An ARM makes sense when your circumstances align with the fixed period. Here's my framework:

Good candidates for a 5/1 or 7/1 ARM right now:

  • You plan to sell the home within 5–7 years (job relocation planned, lifestyle transition expected, or using it as a stepping-stone home)
  • You expect to refinance when rates drop — you're betting on refinancing into a fixed rate around 2028–2029 if the rate environment improves
  • You have significant financial flexibility — even if rates reset higher, you can absorb a $300–$400/month increase without financial strain
  • You're buying a more expensive Collin County home ($450K–$700K) where the ARM savings are larger in absolute dollars

ARM is probably NOT right for you if:

  • You plan to stay in the home 10+ years with no intention of selling or refinancing
  • You're qualifying at the maximum of your DTI — a reset payment increase would create hardship
  • You're a first-time buyer who values payment certainty over monthly savings
  • Your purchase is driven by fear of rates going higher — that's market timing, not a sound financial plan

A Richardson buyer's ARM scenario: $268/month savings, 5-year plan

A couple in Richardson came to me last month with a $395,000 purchase price in mind. Both are engineers at a tech company in Plano and have a solid two-income household. Their plan: live in the home 4–5 years, then upgrade to a larger home in McKinney or Frisco as their family grows.

At 7.40% on a 30-year fixed (5% down = $375,250 loan), their P&I payment would be $2,597/month. At 6.25% on a 5/1 ARM, it's $2,311/month — a savings of $286/month. Over their 5-year plan that's $17,160 in payment savings during the fixed period, plus they plan to sell before the first reset. Even if they stay longer and the rate resets to 8.05%, their payment goes to $2,826/month — still less than if they'd bought a larger home today.

For their specific situation — defined short-term horizon, financial buffer, and clear plan — the ARM was the right call. I've also talked buyers out of ARMs when their situation didn't match. The product isn't right or wrong; the match to the buyer's circumstances is what matters.

How does lender qualification work for an ARM in 2026?

This is where 2026 ARMs are fundamentally safer than pre-2008 ARMs: post-Dodd-Frank ability-to-repay rules require most lenders to qualify ARM borrowers at the fully indexed rate — meaning SOFR + margin (currently ~8.05%) — not just the lower start rate. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac conventional ARM guidelines generally require qualifying at the higher of the fully indexed rate or the start rate plus 2%.

In practice: if I'm pre-approving you for a 5/1 ARM at 6.25%, I'm running your debt-to-income ratio against an 8.05–8.25% payment, not a 6.25% payment. This means you're built to absorb the first reset without the payment shock that triggered mass defaults in 2007–2008. The lower initial payment is a feature for your budget, not a trap your qualification was built around.
