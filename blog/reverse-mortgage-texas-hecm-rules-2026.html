Reverse Mortgage in Texas 2026: HECM Limit, the 12-Day Rule, and What a DFW Home Can Actually Pay Out

Seniors & RetirementBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 20, 20269 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and more of the calls I get this fall are from homeowners over 62 — or their adult children — asking whether a reverse mortgage makes sense now that regular rates are at 7.40% (Freddie Mac, week of October 15, 2026) and a HELOC payment keeps climbing with the prime rate. In 2026 the FHA reverse mortgage — the Home Equity Conversion Mortgage, or HECM — counts home values up to $1,249,125, up from $1,209,750 in 2025. That limit is the same nationwide, and it's 150% of the 2026 conforming loan limit of $832,750.

Texas adds its own layer on top of the federal HECM rules. Our state constitution (Article XVI, Section 50(k)) has a separate list of reverse-mortgage protections — a 12-day waiting period, counseling timing, spousal consent — and most of the national articles skip them. This post covers both layers, shows the math on a real DFW home, and is honest about when a reverse mortgage is the wrong move.

Who qualifies for a reverse mortgage in Texas in 2026?

  • Age: For a HECM, the youngest borrower must be at least 62. A younger spouse can sometimes be protected as an "eligible non-borrowing spouse," but can't be on the loan.
  • Home: Your principal residence — a single-family home, a 2–4 unit property you live in, an FHA-approved condo, or a qualifying manufactured home.
  • Equity: Enough equity that any existing mortgage can be paid off at closing with the reverse mortgage proceeds (or with your own cash).
  • Financial assessment: The lender reviews your credit and your history of paying property taxes and insurance. If it looks shaky, the lender can require a "set-aside" from your proceeds to pay those bills.
  • Counseling: A session with a HUD-approved HECM counselor before you apply in earnest.

What Texas-specific rules apply to a reverse mortgage?

Texas has one of the most protective homestead laws in the country, and reverse mortgages have their own subsection. Every HECM made in Texas has to meet both HUD's rules and these:

The 12-day notice is the one that catches families off guard. If you're planning around a date — paying off a HELOC before a rate reset, for example — build those 12 days, plus counseling, into the timeline.

How much can I get from a reverse mortgage on a DFW home?

The amount you can borrow is called the principal limit. It's the home value (capped at $1,249,125) times a HUD factor that depends on the youngest borrower's age and the "expected rate," which moves with the 10-year Treasury. Higher rates mean lower factors — so with the 10-year around 5.1% this month, proceeds are lower than they were when rates were lower. Older borrowers get higher factors.

Here's an illustration on a $350,000 home in Garland with a 72-year-old borrower and a $40,000 first mortgage left. I'm using a principal limit factor of about 40%, which is in the range I'd expect at today's rates — your actual factor comes from HUD's table on the day the loan is priced.

Two more costs keep running after closing: an annual FHA mortgage insurance premium of 0.5% of the balance and the loan's interest, both added to what you owe instead of being paid monthly. That's why the balance grows over time.

There's also a first-year limit. In the first 12 months you can generally take the greater of 60% of the principal limit, or your "mandatory obligations" (the mortgage payoff and closing costs) plus 10% of the principal limit. In the example, 60% of $140,000 is $84,000; mandatory obligations of $56,000 plus $14,000 is $70,000. The bigger number, $84,000, applies — so after paying off the $56,000 of obligations, about $28,000 is available in year one and the rest after the first anniversary.

If you have strong retirement income and want to keep as much equity as possible for heirs, a HELOC or 50(a)(6) cash-out is usually cheaper. If the problem is the monthly payment itself — the income just isn't there — a HECM is the tool that removes it. My Texas HELOC rules guide covers the 80% cap in detail.

What happens to the house when the borrower dies or moves out?

The loan becomes due when the last borrower dies, sells the home, or is away from it for more than 12 consecutive months (for example, moving into a nursing facility). It can also become due if property taxes, homeowners insurance or HOA dues go unpaid, or the home isn't maintained. In Texas, that last point matters a lot: our property taxes are among the highest in the country, so a reverse mortgage only works if the tax bill keeps getting paid.

When the loan comes due, heirs can keep the home by paying off the balance, or — if the balance is more than the home is worth — paying 95% of the appraised value. They can sell it and keep any equity left over. Or they can hand it back, and because the loan is non-recourse, they owe nothing more. Heirs generally get 30 days to respond and up to six months to sell or refinance, with possible extensions.

Does a reverse mortgage affect my Texas homestead exemption or property taxes?

No — you still own the home, so your homestead exemption stays in place. Texas raised the school homestead exemption to $140,000, and homeowners 65 and older or disabled get an additional $60,000 on top of that, plus a school tax ceiling. See my breakdown of the $140,000 homestead exemption. You remain responsible for paying the tax bill every year.

A Garland homeowner who used a reverse mortgage to drop a payment

A widow in Garland I spoke with this fall — 74, living on Social Security and a small pension — owed about $52,000 on a mortgage with an $890 monthly payment. Her home was worth about $340,000. She'd been pulling from savings every month to cover the payment, property taxes and a rising insurance premium.

A HECM paid off the existing mortgage at closing, eliminated the $890 payment, and left a line of credit for emergencies. We set aside part of her proceeds to cover property taxes and insurance, which the financial assessment required and which she preferred anyway. Before she applied, her daughter joined the HUD counseling session — I always recommend that, because the heirs are the ones who deal with the loan later. In her case, keeping the house mattered more than leaving the full equity to her kids, and they agreed. That's the conversation that has to happen first.

When is a reverse mortgage the wrong choice?

  • You plan to move within a few years — the upfront costs are too high to spread over a short stay.
  • You can't keep up with Texas property taxes and insurance even without a mortgage payment.
  • Leaving the home free and clear to heirs is your top priority.
  • A younger spouse isn't on the loan and you haven't confirmed the non-borrowing spouse protections apply.

If any of those fit, look at other options first — including whether your retirement assets can help you qualify for a regular loan through asset depletion or whether Social Security income with the 2027 COLA supports a refinance. What is the 12-day rule for reverse mortgages in Texas?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329). Under Article XVI, Section 50(k)(9) of the Texas Constitution, a reverse mortgage cannot close until at least 12 days after the lender gives you a separate written notice that both you and the lender sign. Texas also requires your reverse-mortgage counseling to be completed no earlier than 180 days and no later than 5 days before closing. They can also sell and keep any remaining equity, or give the home back, and because a HECM is non-recourse they won't owe the difference.
