K-Shaped Housing Market: How DFW Buyers Who Need a Mortgage Compete With Cash in 2026

Market UpdateBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 16, 20268 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer based in Garland, and on October 15, 2026 CNN ran a story describing the U.S. housing market as "K-shaped" — higher-income and cash buyers taking a growing share of transactions while buyers who depend on a mortgage step back. The numbers behind that framing are real. Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey put the 30-year fixed at 7.40% for the week of October 15, unchanged from the week before and the highest level since November 2023. The 15-year averaged 6.71%. A year earlier the 30-year sat at 6.27%. Over at the Mortgage Bankers Association, purchase applications came in 16% below the same week last year, and the MBA's own contract rate reading hit 7.50% for the week ended October 9.

Here's the part the national coverage leaves out: a thinner buyer pool is not only bad news for you. Fewer financed buyers in the market means fewer competing offers on the Garland, Mesquite and Rockwall listings I see every week. The problem is not that you can't win — it's that when you do run into a cash offer, you need to know which levers actually move a seller. I've spent 2026 writing financed offers that beat cash, and almost all of it comes down to two things: certainty and Texas contract mechanics. Let me show you both.

What does a "K-shaped housing market" actually mean for me?

The K is a shape describing two groups moving in opposite directions at the same time. The upper arm is buyers with equity, cash, or household income high enough that a 7.40% rate is an annoyance rather than a barrier — they keep transacting. The lower arm is first-time buyers and anyone whose purchasing power is set by a monthly payment. Morning Consult and TD Securities data cited in the CNN piece showed households earning $100,000 or more growing more likely to say they want to buy, while lower-income consumers pulled back.

What that means practically in DFW: you are less likely to face eight competing offers than you were in 2021, but the offers you do face are more likely to be strong — cash, large down payments, or relocation buyers with corporate help. And when a seller gets a cash offer alongside yours, they discount for the certainty. NerdWallet, citing Cotality data, reported that in 2025 sellers accepted roughly a 9% discount on all-cash purchases compared with financed offers. On a $350,000 Garland home, that gap is about $31,500 of perceived value you have to make up with something other than price.

You make most of it up with terms. Not all of it with money.

What is a fully underwritten pre-approval, and why does it beat a normal letter?

Most pre-approval letters in circulation are generated after a soft look at a credit report and a conversation about income. They are fine for touring homes and nearly worthless as a competitive weapon, because the listing agent knows nothing has been verified.

A fully underwritten pre-approval is a different document. I collect your full file up front — paystubs, W-2s or returns, two months of complete bank statements, and a hard credit pull — and send it through underwriting before you have a property. An underwriter issues an approval with conditions that come down to the appraisal, the title commitment and a final employment check. When I hand that to a listing agent, I can say on the phone: this file is underwritten, the only open items are the appraisal and title, and we can fund in 21 days. That sentence is the single most valuable thing I do for a buyer in a market like this one, and it costs you nothing extra.

How do I use the Texas Third Party Financing Addendum to my advantage?

This is where the national advice articles get Texas wrong, and it matters.

Almost every "how to beat a cash offer" page tells you to waive contingencies. In Texas, the financing protection lives in the Third Party Financing Addendum (the TPFA), and the deadline in it is usually set at 21 days. If your loan is not approved by that deadline, you can terminate and your earnest money comes back. Waiving the TPFA entirely is a bad trade. You hand the seller the right to keep your earnest money if the loan dies for any reason, and in exchange you get a bullet point on a form.

The better lever is shortening it. When your file is already underwritten, a 10-to-14-day approval deadline is honest — I can actually hit it — and to the seller it reads almost like no contingency at all. You keep your protection. They get their certainty. That one change, written correctly, has won more listings for my buyers this year than any amount of extra earnest money.

Two related Texas points worth knowing, because they confuse buyers constantly: your option fee and earnest money are two separate payments, and the option period is a paid, unrestricted right to terminate that exists independently of the financing addendum. Don't give up the option period to look tough — it's the cheapest protection in the contract.

What else makes a financed offer look stronger in DFW right now?

  • Larger earnest money. Moving from 1% to 2–3% signals you are not going to walk. On a $350,000 home that's $7,000–$10,500 instead of $3,500 — and it is credited to you at closing, not lost.
  • Appraisal gap language with a cap. Rather than a blanket promise, state a dollar figure: you'll cover up to $10,000 of any shortfall between the sales price and the appraised value. Capped language protects you and still removes the seller's main appraisal fear. If an appraisal does come in low, I walk you through every option before you write a check.
  • Flexible closing and leaseback. If the seller needs to stay 10 days after funding, offer it. Owner-occupant buyers can accommodate a leaseback far more easily than an investor can, which is a genuine edge over institutional cash.
  • Ask for a rate buydown instead of a price cut. In DFW's current market a large share of closings include seller concessions. Converting those dollars into discount points usually buys you more monthly relief than the equivalent price reduction.

Do I qualify to make this kind of offer?

The requirements for a fully underwritten pre-approval are the same as for the loan itself — you are simply doing the work earlier:

  • Credit: 580+ for FHA at 3.5% down, 620+ for conventional. Higher scores price better at every tier.
  • Income documentation: 30 days of paystubs and two years of W-2s, or two years of returns plus year-to-date profit and loss if you're self-employed.
  • Assets: two months of complete bank statements — every page, including the blank ones.
  • Debt-to-income: generally up to 50% on FHA with strong compensating factors, commonly 45–50% on conventional.
  • Timeline: give me about 10 business days before you want to write offers.

A real example from this fall. I worked with a couple in Mesquite — combined income about $112,000, scores in the 690s, 5% down on a $345,000 listing. They had already lost one house to a cash investor offering $8,000 less than they did. We spent nine days getting the file fully underwritten, then rewrote on a different property: same price as a competing cash offer, 14-day TPFA deadline, 2.5% earnest money, $10,000 capped appraisal gap, and a 10-day free leaseback because the sellers were closing on a build in Forney. The sellers took their offer over cash that was $5,000 higher. The listing agent told me directly it was the shortened financing deadline that did it — she had never seen one at 14 days backed by an underwriter's approval.
