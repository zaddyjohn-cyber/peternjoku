What the October 2026 Housing Executive Order Actually Does for Texas Homeowners

Market NewsBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 30, 20269 min read

On October 22, 2026 the President signed Executive Order 14438, "Decreasing Housing Costs for the American People." Within a day I had three clients forward me headlines about it, two of them asking whether they should pause a refinance to wait for lower costs.

So I did something the coverage mostly didn't: I read the order itself, all the way through, rather than the write-ups about it. I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland (NMLS #2670329). What I found is that the two pages ranking above the order for this topic both overstate it — one trade outlet says the order "calls for" appraisal waivers and fee cuts when the text says the Director shall consider them, and a major trade association describes a mortgage-insurance rule that is close to the opposite of what federal law already does. Those distinctions decide whether you should wait or act. Here is the order as written.

What did the October 2026 housing executive order actually do?

An executive order directs federal agencies. It is not a statute and it does not rewrite your loan documents. In this one, some items are commands and some are requests to study — and the difference is the whole story. The verb in each section:

What the order addressesSectionThe verb in the textMandated?
Expand the title acceptance pilot into an ongoing program4(a)"shall, if he determines … direct"Yes, if the Director so determines
Raise that pilot above its 80% LTV limit4(b)(i)"shall consider"No
Remove the pilot's geographic limits4(b)(ii)"shall consider"No
Waive appraisals, income re-verification, documentation on limited cash-out refis4(b)(iii)"shall consider"No
Reduce or eliminate loan-level price adjustments and other fees on those refis4(b)(iv)"shall consider"No

Count the "consider" rows. Every single one of the changes a refinancing homeowner would care about most — no appraisal, no income documents, lower fees — sits in that column. And note the one deadline in the entire order: the 180 days in Section 3(b), for manufactured-housing recommendations, which lands around April 20, 2027. Nothing else has a date on it.

Will this order lower my mortgage rate?

No. Not one line of it addresses interest rates on the loans almost all of my clients use.

And this matters because of what happened six days later. On October 28, 2026 the Federal Open Market Committee held the federal funds target at 3.75%–4.00% on a 10–2 vote, with both dissenters wanting a quarter-point increase. Market pricing after that meeting pointed to a genuine chance of a hike in December, not a cut. Meanwhile the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey 30-year average came in at 7.24% for the week of October 29 — down from 7.32% and 7.40% in the two prior weeks, and the lowest reading of the month, but still roughly a point above a year ago.

So the rate is doing its own thing, and no executive order moves it. Here is the scale of what a rate does versus what a fee does, on a $350,000 loan:

  • $350,000 at 7.24%: about $2,385 a month in principal and interest.
  • The same loan half a point lower, at 6.74%: about $2,268 — a $117 a month difference, around $1,400 a year.
  • Every closing-cost item this order touches, combined: a one-time figure in the hundreds to low thousands.

That is not an argument to ignore closing costs. It is an argument against postponing a decision that pencils today in the hope that a fee changes later.

What does the order change about title insurance on a refinance?

This is the most concrete part. Section 4(a) directs FHFA, if the Director determines it appropriate, to turn Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's title acceptance pilot into an ongoing program. That pilot lets certain low-risk refinances close without a traditional lender's title policy, using an attorney opinion letter or the lender's own title assessment instead. The numbers reported so far: Fannie has run roughly 8,000 loans on attorney opinion letters and about 22,000 through title acceptance with no title defects reported, saving an estimated $25 million over about 18 months, or roughly $500 to $1,500 per loan. Freddie's equivalent has averaged $600 to $650 per transaction, in a range of $400 to $1,700, across more than $3 billion of volume.

The counterargument is real and comes from the title industry: refinances are only about 7% of title premiums, but fraud and forgery claims on refinances average close to $207,000 apiece. A thin file is cheap until it isn't.

The Texas angle nobody writes about: our title premiums are promulgated by the Texas Department of Insurance, not negotiated. So unlike a borrower in a state with shopped title pricing, you can look up to the dollar what the lender's policy on your refinance costs before anyone waives anything — I walk through the published rate structure in what Texas title insurance actually costs. A waiver here removes a known number, not a mystery fee. That also means the savings are predictable, and modest relative to your rate.

Does this mean my PMI gets cancelled automatically?

You already have cancellation rights, and they are stronger than most homeowners realize. Under the Homeowners Protection Act of 1998, private mortgage insurance on a conventional loan:

TriggerThresholdWho starts it
Automatic termination78% of original valueThe servicer, on its own
Borrower request, 2–5 years of seasoningbelow 75%You
Borrower request, more than 5 yearsbelow 80%You

I flag this because one widely circulated summary of the order said homeowners "must request" release at 78% — which gets it backwards. At 78% it is supposed to happen without you asking. The request thresholds are the other ones.

What Section 2 adds: FHFA is directed to have Fannie and Freddie build a pilot that cancels MI proactively once equity thresholds are hit, to make servicers notify borrowers promptly, and to cut out unnecessary documentation and appraisal costs in the cancellation process. Those last two are genuinely useful — the appraisal fee is the usual reason people give up on an early cancellation request.

The catch is in the applicability language: the pilot covers mortgages made after the pilot is established. If you closed in 2026, it is not your pilot. Which is why I'd rather you act on the rights you have now — I lay out the appraisal-versus-AVM route in cancelling PMI after your home appreciates.

Does any of this help a Texas cash-out refinance?

Here is where Texas law overrides federal enthusiasm, and where every national article on this order falls silent.

Section 4(b) aims its relief at limited cash-out refinances — the kind where you are lowering a payment, not pulling equity out. In Texas, those are usually a Section 50(f)(2) rate-and-term refinance. A Section 50(a)(6) cash-out is a different instrument under our constitution, with its own 12-day notice, its 80% loan-to-value ceiling, and its three-day right of rescission. The order's refi package does not reach it.

And even if FHFA does everything Section 4(b) asks it to consider, a Texas 50(a)(6) can never take an appraisal waiver, because the Selling Guide requires an appraisal attached to the written acknowledgment of fair market value that you sign. I covered that mechanic in my piece on appraisal waivers and value acceptance in Texas, and the two loan types side by side in the 50(f)(2) rules and my Texas cash-out guide.

One more correction worth making: Section 5's rate reduction applies to USDA and VA direct loans, which the agencies make themselves. The VA and USDA loans I close across DFW are guaranteed loans funded by a lender. If you are buying in Forney or Royse City with zero down, your rate is a market rate and Section 5 does not touch it.

What should I actually do between now and spring?

Last week a homeowner in Garland asked me exactly the right question. He has a $300,000 balance on a loan he closed early this year at 7.875% — about $2,175 a month in principal and interest. At today's 7.24% his payment would be about $2,044, a saving of roughly $131 a month. His estimated costs to refinance came to about $5,500, which puts his break-even near 42 months.

His question: should he wait for the executive order to cut the title piece? Run it both ways. If a title waiver eventually took around $1,500 off, his costs fall to roughly $4,000 and his break-even shortens to about 31 months. That is a real improvement — worth about eleven months of break-even. But it is contingent on an FHFA determination with no deadline attached, and his loan has to still make sense at whatever rate exists when it arrives. Meanwhile every month he waits at 7.875% costs him that $131.

What I told him, and what I'd tell you:

  1. Decide on today's numbers. If the break-even works now, do it now. If it doesn't, the order's savings won't rescue it.
  2. Check your PMI against the 78% line this month. That right exists today and costs nothing to ask about.
  3. Ask whether yours is a 50(f)(2) or a 50(a)(6) before you assume any of the refi relief could apply to you.
  4. Watch for FHFA actually acting — an announcement establishing the MI pilot or expanding title acceptance. Until then, Section 4(b) is a to-do list, not a benefit.
  5. Don't let a fee decision drive a rate decision. The rate is worth roughly ten times the fee over any holding period that matters.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and not because of the order — but you may already have more rights than you think. Under the Homeowners Protection Act of 1998, PMI on a conventional loan terminates automatically when the balance reaches 78% of the original value, and you can request cancellation earlier, generally below 75% with two to five years of seasoning or below 80% after five years. Section 2 of the order directs FHFA to build a pilot that cancels it more proactively, but the order applies that pilot to mortgages made after the pilot is established — so it does nothing for a loan you closed in 2026.
