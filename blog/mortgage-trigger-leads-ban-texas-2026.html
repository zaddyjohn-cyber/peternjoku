Why Am I Getting Calls After Applying for a Mortgage? The 2026 Trigger Leads Ban Explained for Texas Buyers

Credit & PrivacyBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 19, 20268 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and until this year the most common complaint I heard from first-time buyers had nothing to do with rates. It was this: "I applied with you on Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning I had 30 calls and 14 texts from lenders I never heard of." That flood had a name — trigger leads — and as of early March 2026 most of it is now illegal under federal law. The Homebuyers Privacy Protection Act (H.R. 2808) was signed on September 5, 2025 and took effect 180 days later; the National Association of Mortgage Brokers dates it to March 5, 2026.

But some of my clients in Garland, Mesquite and Rockwall still get calls after a credit pull, and they want to know why. This post covers what changed, what didn't, why your phone might still ring, and the exact steps I give buyers before I pull credit so their home search stays quiet. Most of the pages ranking for this question were written before the law took effect and still describe trigger leads as legal and unlimited — they are out of date.

What is a mortgage trigger lead?

When a lender pulls your credit for a mortgage, the inquiry shows up at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion with a code saying it's a mortgage inquiry. For years the bureaus packaged that event — your name, contact details and the fact that you were actively shopping for a home loan — and sold it as a "trigger" to other lenders, brokers and lead resellers. Buyers of those lists could reach you within minutes. One bank reported customers getting calls about 20 minutes after their credit was pulled.

Your lender did not sell your information. The credit bureau did, and the lender who pulled your credit had no way to stop it. Changing your phone number on the application didn't help either, because the bureaus used the contact information already on your credit file.

What did the 2026 trigger leads ban actually change?

The law amends the Fair Credit Reporting Act. A credit bureau can now furnish a mortgage trigger lead only in narrow situations:

  • Existing relationship — the company receiving the lead already has a qualifying financial relationship with you, like your current mortgage servicer, your bank, or the broker you're already working with.
  • You opted in — you affirmatively agreed to receive these solicitations.
  • Firm offer only — even when allowed, the lead must be used for a bona fide firm offer of credit or insurance, not general marketing.

Why am I still getting calls after my mortgage credit check?

This is where the out-of-date pages get it wrong. If your phone is still busy after a 2026 application, it's almost always one of these six reasons:

  1. You opted in somewhere. Rate-comparison and "see your rate in 60 seconds" websites usually include consent language that lets several lenders contact you. That's not a trigger lead — you volunteered. It's the most common source I see.
  2. Existing relationships. Your current mortgage servicer, your bank, or your credit union can still reach out, and many do the moment they see you shopping.
  3. Prescreened mail offers. Those "you're pre-approved" letters come from prescreened credit lists, which work differently from trigger leads and continue unless you opt out.
  4. Non-mortgage credit. If you also applied for a car loan or a credit card — which I hope you didn't during a home purchase — those inquiries are outside the mortgage ban.
  5. Old data. Contact lists bought before March 2026 are still floating around in call centers.
  6. Plain spam and spoofing. Robocallers don't follow the Fair Credit Reporting Act. They dial number blocks and get lucky.

How do I stop mortgage solicitation calls before I apply?

I give every buyer this checklist before I pull credit. It takes about ten minutes:

  1. Opt out of prescreened offers at OptOutPrescreen.com, the official site run by the consumer credit bureaus. Online opt-out lasts five years; mailing in the signed form makes it permanent.
  2. Register your cell on the National Do Not Call Registry at donotcall.gov.
  3. Read the consent box on any rate website before you hit submit. If it says "you agree to be contacted by up to X lenders," that's your phone number being distributed.
  4. Pick your lenders first, then let them pull. Shop by talking to two or three loan officers, then let only those lenders pull credit.
  5. Don't freeze and forget. A credit freeze blocks new account fraud, but it also blocks my credit pull. If you have freezes in place, lift them temporarily at all three bureaus for the day of your application.

How can I tell a scam call from my real lender during a Texas closing?

This matters more than the annoyance. When strangers know you're buying, some try to impersonate your lender or title company. In Texas, closings run through a title company, and wire fraud aimed at buyers' cash-to-close is the costliest version of this. My rules:

  • I will never ask you to wire money. Wiring instructions come from your title company, and you confirm them by calling a number you look up yourself — never one from the email.
  • Your real lender already has your application. Anyone asking you to "re-verify" your Social Security number on an inbound call is not me.
  • "Your rate lock is expiring today unless you switch to us" is a pressure tactic. A real lock extension conversation happens with the lender that issued the lock.

Can I still shop multiple lenders without damaging my credit?

Yes — and now it costs you less peace and quiet than it used to. Credit scoring models treat several mortgage inquiries made close together as one shopping event. Mortgage lending still uses older classic FICO versions, which use a shorter grouping window than newer scores, so I tell DFW buyers to finish all rate shopping within 14 days. On a $350,000 loan at 7.40%, the payment is about $2,423 principal and interest; a quote just 0.125% lower saves roughly $30 a month, or about $10,800 over 30 years. That's worth two or three phone calls — compare Loan Estimates on the same day with the same lock period and the same points so you're comparing apples to apples.

A Garland buyer, before and after the ban

In late 2025, a first-time buyer I worked with in Garland — 690 credit score, FHA loan on a $285,000 home — counted 41 calls in the three days after her credit was pulled. Two callers told her I had "sent her file over" to them, which wasn't true, and it shook her confidence enough that she nearly walked away. This spring I helped her younger brother buy in Mesquite. We did the OptOutPrescreen and Do Not Call steps the week before his application, and after the March 2026 law took effect he got exactly two calls: his own bank, which he already had an account with, and one obvious robocall. Same family, same kind of loan, very different experience.
