Buying a House With Tenants Still Living In It: A Texas Buyer's Guide

Buying ProcessBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 30, 20269 min read

A lot of small rental stock is hitting the DFW market right now, which means I am seeing this question far more than I used to: the house is perfect, the price is right, and there is somebody living in it who is not the seller.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland (NMLS #2670329). Two things are true at the same time, and the whole problem lives in the gap between them. First, in Texas a buyer takes title subject to the existing lease — the tenancy does not evaporate at closing. Second, if you are financing this as your home, your loan carries an occupancy deadline. The pages ranking for this question are written for investors, and the one that mentions Texas does it in about three general sentences. Nobody addresses the buyer who actually wants to live there. Let me do both.

Can I buy a house in Texas that already has tenants in it?

Yes, and people do it constantly. The question is as what. There are really two transactions hiding under one listing, and they have different loans, different down payments and different rules.

Sort yourself into a column before you do anything else. Most of the trouble I see comes from buyers who want the left column's terms and the right column's tenant.

Does the lease end when the house sells?

No. This is the part sellers' agents sometimes get wrong, and it is settled in Texas: a buyer takes the property subject to the existing lease. If a tenant has seven months left on a fixed-term lease, they generally have the right to stay those seven months, at the rent in that lease, on the terms in that lease. You inherit the landlord's side of the contract, including anything the seller promised in writing — and anything promised verbally that the tenant claims.

A month-to-month tenancy is a different and much easier situation. Under Texas Property Code §91.001 a monthly tenancy can be terminated by either the landlord or the tenant giving notice; where rent is paid monthly, that works out to about one month's notice. A sale by itself does not terminate it — somebody still has to give proper notice, and after closing that somebody is you. Have a Texas attorney confirm the notice your specific lease requires; some leases contract for more.

So the practical sequence for an owner-occupant is: read the lease during the option period, find the end date, and compare it to your occupancy deadline. That comparison decides the deal. My post on earnest money and the option fee covers why that window is the only place you have leverage.

I want to live in it — how soon do I have to move in?

The standard expectation on a primary-residence loan is that you occupy the home within 60 days of closing. That is not a soft guideline. It appears in your note, and you sign an occupancy certification at the closing table affirming your intent to live there.

Which creates the collision. You cannot truthfully certify that you will occupy a home in 60 days if a tenant holds a legal right to live in it for seven months. And you cannot evict your way out of it — the tenant has done nothing wrong, and their lease is enforceable against you.

The fix is in the contract, not in underwriting. Underwriting has no mechanism to bless this; it can only decline it. Your realistic options:

  1. Seller delivers the property vacant at closing. Cleanest by far. The seller negotiates with their tenant — a cash-for-keys arrangement, an early termination agreement — and possession at closing is vacant. Put it in the contract, with an estoppel certificate confirming the tenant's understanding.
  2. Time the closing after the lease expires. If the lease ends in six weeks, close in seven and the problem disappears.
  3. Buy it as an investment property and move in when the lease ends. Honest, allowable, and more expensive — 20–25% down, reserves, investment pricing. If you later occupy it, you occupy it; you just didn't finance it on a false certification.
  4. Walk. During the option period this costs you the option fee. Afterwards it costs a great deal more.

What you must not do is finance it as a primary residence and plan to sort the tenant out afterwards. Occupancy misrepresentation is loan fraud, and the signature on the affidavit is yours.

Who owes the tenant's security deposit after closing?

You do — and faster than you'd expect. This is the most expensive thing buyers in this situation get wrong, and no page ranking for this question cites the statute.

Under Texas Property Code §92.105(a), the new owner is liable for the return of the security deposit from the date title to the premises is acquired, regardless of whether anyone gave the tenant notice. Not from the date you receive the money. Not from the date you send a letter. From the date you take title.

Read that against the likely facts: the seller collected a $2,800 deposit three years ago and spent it. If you did not get a credit at closing, the tenant can still come to you for it at move-out. The seller's own path out of liability generally runs through delivering the tenant a signed statement naming the new owner and address and confirming the deposit has been transferred or assumed — different aggregators word §92.105(b) differently, so have your title company and attorney handle the actual language.

What I want to see on your Closing Disclosure before you sign:

  • the full security deposit as a seller credit to you;
  • any pet deposit or last month's rent held, credited separately;
  • prepaid rent prorated — if the tenant paid the full month and you close on the 20th, the remaining days belong to you;
  • a written deposit ledger from the seller, and a transfer letter going to the tenant.

Worked example on a DFW rental closing the 20th of a 31-day month with $1,850 rent: the daily rate is about $59.68, eleven remaining days is roughly $656 of prepaid rent to you, plus a $1,850 deposit credit — about $2,506 that should be on the CD and routinely isn't. I check this line on every tenant-occupied file. My walkthrough of reading your Closing Disclosure shows where it lands.

Can I use the tenant's rent to qualify?

On an investment purchase, usually — with a haircut. Lenders typically count about 75% of the gross rent, holding back roughly a quarter for vacancy and maintenance. Your underwriter makes the final call on treatment, so don't build your budget on the full rent.

What documents that takes:

  • the fully executed lease, all pages, all addenda;
  • a Comparable Rent Schedule — Form 1007 for a single-family home, or Form 1025 for a two-to-four unit property, prepared by the appraiser;
  • an operating income statement (Form 216) where the program calls for one;
  • proof of rent actually paid — bank deposits, a property manager's ledger;
  • a tenant estoppel certificate, signed by the tenant, confirming the rent, the deposit amount, the lease end date, that there are no verbal side agreements, and that they have no outstanding claims against the landlord.

That estoppel certificate is where buried problems surface. I have seen one come back revealing the tenant had been promised a rent reduction in exchange for doing yard work, and another showing the "deposit" was actually two months of free rent.

If the rent is below market — common, because long-term tenants rarely get raised to market — you are generally stuck with the lease rent, not the Form 1007 opinion, until that lease ends. And negative cash flow counts against you: if the rent credit is $1,390 and the payment is $1,590, that $200 a month goes into your debt-to-income ratio as a liability. More on the mechanics in using rental income to qualify and, if the numbers are tight, DSCR loans for Texas investors.

What if the tenant won't let the appraiser in?

It happens, and it can quietly kill your closing date. The appraiser needs interior access. The tenant has no contractual obligation to you, may not have been told the house is selling, and in Texas a landlord generally has to give notice before entering for non-emergency reasons.

What works, in order: have the seller coordinate access, since the tenant's relationship is with them; schedule a specific window rather than a vague day; and build an access obligation into the contract so a refusal is the seller's problem, not yours. Expect a week to ten days for a normal DFW appraisal and add buffer here. If the appraiser can only see the exterior, you are usually looking at a subject-to-interior-inspection report and another delay.

What does this look like with real numbers?

This spring I worked with a buyer who found a $280,000 house in Mesquite listed below the comparable sales, with a tenant seven months into a twelve-month lease at $1,750 a month. She wanted it as her home.

We ran both columns. As a primary residence it was a non-starter on the lease alone — five months left against a 60-day occupancy requirement. As an investment property she needed 20% down, about $56,000, plus reserves, which she didn't have. So we went at the contract instead: her agent negotiated that the seller would deliver the property vacant at closing, the seller paid the tenant a relocation sum to leave two months early, and we closed 41 days later with the house empty.

She put 10% down — a $252,000 loan. At 7.24%, the Freddie Mac survey average for the week of October 29, 2026, principal and interest came to about $1,717 a month. With Dallas County taxes near 2.0% (about $467), insurance around $190, and private mortgage insurance of roughly $105 until she reaches 80%, her payment landed close to $2,479. The deposit and prepaid rent were moot once the property delivered vacant — but we checked the CD anyway.

The whole deal turned on one thing: we read the lease on day two of the option period instead of day twenty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I buy a house with an FHA loan if a tenant is still living there?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and yes — but only if the tenant will be out in time for you to occupy the home. FHA, VA, USDA and conventional primary-residence loans all expect you to move in promptly after closing, generally within 60 days, and you sign an occupancy certification saying you will. A fixed-term lease that runs past that window is a real conflict, because in Texas the buyer takes title subject to the existing lease. The fix is almost always in the contract, not in underwriting: either the seller delivers the property vacant at closing or the lease expires inside your window. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 with the lease dates and I'll tell you quickly.

How soon do I have to move in if I buy a tenant-occupied house?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and for a primary-residence loan the standard expectation is that you occupy the home within 60 days of closing. That deadline isn't a suggestion — it lives in your note and in the occupancy affidavit you sign at the closing table, and representing that you'll occupy a home you can't legally enter is a serious problem. So count the days on the lease before you count on the loan. If the lease has seven months left, you are either buying it as an investment property, with the higher down payment and reserves that go with it, or you are renegotiating possession. Call or text me at 469-545-7180.

Who is responsible for the tenant's security deposit after I buy the house in Texas?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and this one surprises almost every buyer: under Texas Property Code §92.105 the new owner is liable for returning the security deposit from the date title is acquired — whether or not anyone gave the tenant notice. So if the seller pocketed the deposit and you didn't get a credit at closing, you can still owe the tenant that money. Make sure the deposit and any prepaid rent show up as a seller credit on your Closing Disclosure, and get the ledger in writing. The seller's own release from liability generally runs through delivering the tenant a signed statement naming the new owner. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and I'll check your CD before you sign.

Can I use the tenant's rent to help me qualify for the mortgage?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and on an investment purchase usually yes, but not the whole amount. Lenders typically count about 75% of the gross rent, holding back roughly a quarter for vacancy and maintenance, and they document it with the fully executed lease plus a Comparable Rent Schedule — Form 1007 for a single-family home, Form 1025 for a two-to-four unit — and often an operating income statement. If the rent is below market, you are usually stuck with the actual lease rent, and the shortfall against your payment counts against your debt-to-income ratio. On a primary residence you generally get no rental income credit at all. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 to run it.
