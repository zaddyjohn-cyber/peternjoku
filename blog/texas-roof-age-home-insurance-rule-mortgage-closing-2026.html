Texas Roof Age Home Insurance Rule 2026: What It Means for Your Closing

InsuranceBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 27, 20267 min read

On September 29, 2026, the Texas Department of Insurance published something that matters more to DFW homebuyers than almost anything else that came out of Austin this fall. TDI said it is clarifying its rule that, in the department's own words, "insurance companies can't decline or not renew a home insurance policy based on the age of a house or its roof." Insurance Commissioner Amanda Crawford put it this way: "Texans deserve fair access to home insurance, no matter the age of their home."

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer based in Garland (NMLS #2670329), and I care about this for one reason that has nothing to do with insurance premiums. If you cannot get a policy, your loan cannot fund. Not "it gets harder." It does not fund. I have watched clean, fully approved files sit on the funding desk because a carrier would not write the house, and in hail country that happens more than buyers expect. So let me explain exactly what this clarification does, what it very deliberately does not do, and where insurance sits in your Texas contract timeline.

What exactly did TDI change in September 2026?

TDI framed it as a clarification of an existing rule rather than a brand-new prohibition. The release says carriers "can't deny or not renew a policy simply because the house or any of these materials are older." The important half is the part most coverage skipped: carriers may still base a decision on the home's physical condition, including its components.

That distinction is the whole ballgame. Age is now off the table as a standalone reason. Condition is not. A thirty-year-old roof that is sound, sealed and free of damage is supposed to be underwritten on what it is, not on its birthday. A thirty-year-old roof with lifted shingles, exposed decking and two prior hail claims is a condition file, and the clarification does not help you there.

One honest caveat, because I would rather you hear it from me than find out later: the TDI release I read does not state an effective date, a docket number or a comment period, and the rule document it links is a proposal file. Treat this as the department's stated position as of September 29, 2026, and have your agent confirm current status with the carrier in writing. I am a loan officer, not your insurance agent, and this is not legal or insurance advice.

If you are told no and the reason you are given sits in the first two rows, that is precisely the situation TDI addressed. Ask for it in writing. A verbal "we don't do old roofs" from a call center is not a declination you should accept at face value three weeks before closing.

Why does homeowners insurance decide whether my loan funds?

Here is the chain, and I want it in plain order because almost no article about roof age ever connects it:

  1. Your lender requires hazard insurance with dwelling coverage adequate to rebuild, and requires being named as mortgagee on the policy.
  2. The policy has to be bound and paid (usually the first year's premium, collected at closing) before the file goes to funding.
  3. No bound policy means no closing disclosure that balances, no funding authorization, and no keys.

That is why I treat the insurance quote as an early-contract task, not a closing-week errand. Credit score, reserves, and a 20% down payment do not substitute for an insurable roof. This is also why insurance belongs on your radar separately from your financing contingency: the Third Party Financing Addendum protects your financing approval, and the option period gives you inspection leverage, but neither one is an insurance contingency. Insurability is its own risk sitting quietly between them.

How much does the premium change my monthly payment?

The premium is escrowed, which means it is part of PITI, which means it is part of the debt-to-income ratio that decides how much house you qualify for. Let me show it on a real DFW number.

Take a $350,000 home in Garland with 20% down — a $280,000 loan. At 7.32%, the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey 30-year average for the week of October 22, 2026 (down from 7.40% the prior week, and 6.19% a year earlier), the principal and interest works out to about $1,923 a month:

That $75 a month is not just a budget annoyance. On a buyer running a tight back-end ratio, $75 of extra PITI can cost roughly $10,000–$11,000 of purchase price at this rate. It is the same arithmetic I walk through in my piece on how DFW homeowners insurance affects your mortgage payment, and it cuts the other way too — TDI's rate filings this fall averaged about a 4.3% decrease, which I covered in Texas homeowners insurance rates dropping and what it does to your escrow.

Does roof age still matter even if they have to write the policy?

Yes, and this is the trap. The clarification is about access to a policy. It does not dictate how generously that policy pays a claim. Carriers commonly handle older roofs through endorsements that settle on actual cash value rather than full replacement cost, which means depreciation comes out of your check when the next hailstorm rolls through Rockwall or Mesquite. Two policies can both be issued and still be wildly different products.

So when you compare quotes, do not just compare the premium. Ask one question: is roof loss settled at replacement cost or actual cash value, and is there a separate wind/hail deductible? In DFW that single answer is worth more than a $200 premium difference.

Do I qualify — and when should I get the quote?

Order the insurance quote in the first week of your contract, during the option period, at the same time you order the inspection. Here is the schedule I give my buyers:

  • Days 1–3: General inspection ordered. Insurance quotes requested from at least three carriers using the actual address, roof age and claims history.
  • Days 3–7: If any carrier balks, get the written reason and order a licensed roof inspection with a repair estimate. This is when you still have option-period leverage to ask the seller for repair or credit.
  • Before the option period ends: You should know that the house is insurable at a number you can live with. If it isn't, that is what the option fee bought you.
  • Through underwriting: Send me the declarations page as soon as it's issued so it clears as a condition rather than becoming a funding-day scramble.

Last spring I had a buyer under contract on a 1990s one-story in north Garland at $350,000, putting 20% down, mid-700s credit, about as clean a file as I underwrite. The original-era composition roof was intact, and their first carrier still came back with a no. We asked for the reason in writing, got a licensed roof report showing no active damage, and placed the policy with a different carrier at $3,300 a year instead of the $2,400 we had modeled. Their payment landed at about $2,781 all in rather than $2,706, their ratios still worked, and they closed on the original date. The only reason it wasn't a crisis is that we found out in week one instead of week four.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a Texas insurance company deny my home policy because of the roof age?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and under the clarification the Texas Department of Insurance published on September 29, 2026, insurance companies can't decline or not renew a home insurance policy based on the age of a house or its roof. What a carrier can still do is make a decision based on the home's physical condition, including the condition of the roof itself. So an original 1994 roof that is intact is a different conversation than a 1994 roof with cracked shingles and prior hail damage. If a carrier tells you no and the only reason given is the year the roof was installed, ask for that reason in writing. Will my mortgage still close if I can't get homeowners insurance?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and no — it won't. Every lender I work with requires a bound homeowners policy with the lender listed as mortgagee before funds are released, so no policy means no funding no matter how strong your credit, your down payment or your income are. This is the part buyers underestimate: insurance is not a formality at the end, it's a funding condition. That's why I ask for an insurance quote during the option period rather than two weeks before closing. What should I do if an insurer declines coverage before my Texas closing?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and I'd do four things in this order.
