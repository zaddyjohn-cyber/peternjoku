The New Texas Water Notice (TREC 61-0) and What Your Lender Does With a Seller's Disclosure

ClosingBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 27, 20268 min read

On July 1, 2026 Texas real estate picked up a brand-new disclosure form, and the entire internet wrote about it for sellers and agents. TREC Form 61-0, "Seller's Disclosure About Groundwater and Surface Water Rights," now hangs off new Paragraph 7(I) of the One to Four Family Residential Contract. At the same time the Seller's Disclosure Notice itself got longer, adding questions about insurance coverage, private road maintenance, above-ground storage tanks and conservation easements.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland (NMLS #2670329), and I went looking for a single page explaining what a lender does with all of this. There isn't one. Every page ranking for these forms is a line-by-line walkthrough written for sellers, brokers or exam candidates. Not one of them tells you that three of the four new disclosure items can turn into conditions that hold up your funding. So that is the article I'm writing.

What is TREC Form 61-0 and when does a seller fill it out?

The Water Notice is a standalone form, separate from the Seller's Disclosure Notice. TREC created it following the Sunset Advisory Commission's special-purpose review of the commission's disclosure requirements. Copies in circulation carry a 05-04-2026 form date — commentary puts TREC's adoption on May 4, 2026 — and TREC lists it with an effective date of 07/01/2026.

It is trigger-based. Guidance circulated by Texas title companies describes five threshold questions, and if the seller answers "no" to all five, they are not required to complete the form at all. One of those triggers is whether the seller is aware of a water well on the property. The form also asks whether the seller owns surface water rights, which in Texas means a permit, certified filing or certificate of adjudication administered through the state's environmental commission.

Is the Water Notice actually required?

Here is the nuance that is in TREC's own FAQ and in almost none of the coverage. The Seller's Disclosure About Groundwater and Surface Water Rights is not required to be provided by another law. Compare that to the Seller's Disclosure Notice, which Texas Property Code § 5.008 does require for most sellers of one-to-four-unit residential property, delivered before or at the contract.

So the 61-0's teeth come entirely from the TREC contract. One brokerage guide puts it bluntly: "we cannot require sellers to complete it." That means two practical things for you as a buyer. A missing 61-0 is a contract conversation, not a statutory violation. And if there is a well on the property, you want that form completed anyway — because the information on it is exactly what my underwriter is going to ask about.

What changed on the Seller's Disclosure Notice in 2026?

The revision added items on insurance and windstorm coverage, private road maintenance obligations, above-ground storage tanks over 500 gallons, and conservation easements. Separately, permanently installed and built-in generators were added to Paragraph 2B of the contract as Improvements.

And the flood questions, which have been there since September 1, 2019, remain the most consequential part of the form: floodplain status using FEMA definitions, whether a prior flood-related insurance claim was filed, whether FEMA aid was received, and water damage not caused by a flood event. Worth repeating because sellers get this wrong constantly — repairs do not extinguish the duty. A fully remediated flood is still a material fact, and answering "no" when you know the history is a misrepresentation.

Why does the timing of the disclosure matter so much?

Because of a sequence almost nobody points out. The Seller's Disclosure Notice arrives before or at the contract. The appraisal doesn't get ordered until after your financing approval starts moving. That gap — usually one to two weeks — is free information.

A buyer who reads page two of that disclosure properly knows about the private road, the well, and the 1,000-gallon propane tank while the option period is still running. That is when a seller will fix things, credit you, or let you walk for the price of the option fee. A buyer who skims it finds out when the appraisal comes back with conditions, which is week four, when the only tools left are an extension request and a hard conversation. My post on earnest money and the option fee covers what that window actually buys you, and the Third Party Financing Addendum guide explains the separate financing clock.

Do I qualify? A Forney example

This spring I worked with a family buying outside Forney at $400,000 with 20% down — a $320,000 loan. Their seller's disclosure flagged two things their agent nearly skipped past: a water well serving the irrigation system, and a shared gravel drive maintained by three neighbors with nothing in writing.

We caught both on day three of the option period. The well meant a water test; the private drive meant we needed recorded evidence of legal access and a maintenance arrangement my underwriter would accept. The sellers had the access documentation from their own purchase, and the neighbors signed a simple recorded agreement. At 7.32% — the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey 30-year average for the week of October 22, 2026 — their principal and interest ran about $2,198 a month, with Kaufman County taxes near 1.9% adding roughly $633 and insurance about $250, landing them near $3,081 all in. They closed on the original date.

Had we found the private drive in week four instead of week one, that same file would have needed an extension and a lot of goodwill.

Does a prior flood on the seller's disclosure mean I need flood insurance?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and not automatically — but it means we check carefully. What a disclosed flood claim does is tell me to look harder, because a house can flood outside a mapped zone and a carrier will price that history.
