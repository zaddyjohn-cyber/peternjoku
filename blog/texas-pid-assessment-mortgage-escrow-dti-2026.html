Texas PID Assessments: How They Hit Your Mortgage Payment and Your Loan Amount

Property TaxesBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 30, 20269 min read

Two weeks before closing on a new build, a buyer forwarded me a one-page form her builder had slipped into a stack and asked what it was. It was the notice that her house sits in a Public Improvement District, and that an assessment had been levied against it. The form did not say how much. It never does.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland (NMLS #2670329). PIDs are everywhere in the North Texas growth corridors — the Prosper and Celina side of Collin County, the Forney and Royse City corridors east of Dallas — and the pages that rank for this topic are written by agents, about MUDs, with PIDs as the junior partner. The best of them gives the lender side exactly three sentences. But the lender side is where a PID actually costs you money: in your escrow, in your debt-to-income ratio, and in a year-two payment jump nobody warns you about. Here it is.

What is a PID assessment in Texas?

A Public Improvement District is a defined area where a city or county levies a special assessment on the properties inside it to pay for improvements that benefit those properties — roads, water and sewer lines, drainage, landscaping, trails, entry monuments. Bonds get issued, the improvements get built, and the assessment pays the bonds back over a set term.

It is created under Local Government Code Chapter 372 (some are formed under Chapter 382) on a petition from property owners representing at least 50% of the appraised value and either 50% of the owners or 50% of the area, followed by a public hearing. Note what is not in that list: a bond election. Unlike a MUD, a PID does not require voter approval of its bonds.

Which is why you can buy a house in a PID without ever having voted on anything, and why the notice law exists.

How is a PID different from a MUD?

They get lumped together and they behave differently. Here is the comparison, with one correction the ranking pages need: you will see MUDs cited to Water Code Chapter 49 in one place and Chapter 54 in another, and both are right about different things — Chapter 49 carries the notice requirements (§49.452 and §49.4521) while Chapter 54 carries district creation and bond authority.

The prepayment row is the one worth circling. A MUD tax is a tax and you pay it for as long as the district exists. A PID assessment is a debt obligation attached to your lot, and you can retire it. My companion piece on MUD districts and your mortgage payment covers that side.

Does a PID assessment affect how much house I qualify for?

Yes, and this is the number nobody puts in front of buyers. A PID installment goes into your housing payment, and your housing payment drives your debt-to-income ratio — whether the installment is escrowed with your taxes or billed to you separately. Paying it outside escrow does not hide it from the underwriter.

So the right way to express a PID's cost is not dollars per month. It is purchase price you can no longer afford. Take a real buyer profile: $9,000 a month of gross income, a $450 car payment, a 45% back-end ratio, 10% down, Collin County taxes at about 1.5%, insurance around $180, HOA dues of $100, at 7.24% — the Freddie Mac survey average for the week of October 29, 2026.

An $1,800-a-year assessment costs that buyer roughly $20,000 of buying power. That is the conversation to have before you walk a model home, not after. More on how the ratio works in my debt-to-income guide.

And here is the trap specific to PIDs: a pre-approval issued before anyone identified the district will be too high. I have had buyers go under contract at the top of an approval and then watch the file re-underwrite downward when the title commitment surfaced the assessment.

Is the PID escrowed with my property taxes?

Sometimes. It depends on the district and on your servicer, and the honest answer is that treatment varies — which is itself the problem, because "varies" means you have to ask.

Where a PID assessment is collected on the county tax bill, it typically gets escrowed right alongside your taxes and you never see it as a separate item. Where the district or its administrator bills directly, you pay it yourself — annually or semiannually — and your mortgage statement looks deceptively small.

Where it shows up in your paperwork:

  • Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure, the Projected Payments table — if it is escrowed, it should be inside "Estimated Taxes, Insurance & Assessments," and the box below should be checked to show assessments are included.
  • Prepaids and Initial Escrow Payment at Closing — if the servicer is escrowing it, expect months of it collected up front.
  • Nowhere at all, if it is billed directly — which is exactly why buyers miss it. The CD can be silent on an obligation you absolutely owe.

Two questions to ask before closing, in writing: Is this PID collected on the county tax bill? and Is my servicer escrowing the assessment?

Why did my escrow jump the year after I closed?

This is the part no page in the search results mentions, and on new construction it is the most common payment shock in my business.

Your initial escrow gets funded from the best information available at closing, which on a new build is often the prior year's tax bill — for a vacant lot. No house on it. And if the PID assessment was levied after that bill was issued, it isn't in there either. Twelve months later the escrow analysis catches up to two things at once: the improved value of a finished house, and the full annual assessment.

The result is a shortage spread over twelve months plus a higher ongoing escrow figure. I have seen payments move $400 and more on exactly this mechanism. It is not an error and nobody did anything wrong — it is arithmetic catching up. I walk through the whole pattern in why your escrow payment jumped.

What to do about it: ask me to set your escrow from the improved value and the actual service and assessment plan rather than last year's bill. Your payment starts higher and stays there, which is the outcome you want.

What notice is the seller required to give me?

A real one, backed by real remedies. House Bill 1543, passed by the 87th Legislature and signed June 4, 2021, took effect September 1, 2021. It rewrote Texas Property Code §5.014 and added §§5.0141–5.0145. The legislation came out of complaints from North Texas homeowners about builder nondisclosure of PID assessments — which tells you how routinely this was being missed.

What the law requires:

  • §5.014 — the notice itself and its contents, delivered by the seller.
  • §5.0141 — notice before the execution of a binding contract of purchase and sale. Not at closing. Before you sign.
  • The buyer signs the notice, and the signature is evidence of receipt.
  • §5.0143 — a copy with current information is executed by both parties, acknowledged, and recorded in the county deed records at closing.
  • §5.014(c) carries the exemptions. HB 1543 removed the prior exemption for condominium interests and extended the requirement beyond residential to condominiums, commercial, new construction and unimproved property.
  • The PID must file its notice with the city or county on its next annual service plan update, after which the city or county has seven days to file it with the county clerk.

And here is the catch that makes this post necessary: the notice form does not contain a blank for the assessment amount or any payment schedule. The seller, city or county may add that information voluntarily. Many don't. So a buyer can receive a fully compliant notice and still have no idea whether they are looking at $600 a year or $2,400 a year. The number lives in the service and assessment plan, and you have to go get it.

If the notice was late or never came, the remedies differ by which it was — and the two ranking pages state them differently because they are describing different facts:

Can I pay off a PID assessment early?

Generally yes, and this is a genuine lever a MUD does not give you. Prepaying the assessment retires your lot's share of the bonds and saves the interest and administrative expense you would otherwise pay over the remaining term.

Three things to think about before you do:

  1. What it does to your payment. If the assessment is escrowed, paying it off should remove that line at the next escrow analysis — not immediately. If it is billed directly, the bill simply stops.
  2. What it does to your qualifying. Eliminating a $150 monthly obligation before you buy is worth about $20,000 of purchase price, per the table above. Eliminating it after you close does nothing for the loan you already have.
  3. Whether you'll be there long enough. Prepaying a 20-plus-year obligation three years before you sell is usually a worse use of cash than the down payment or a rate buydown. And an unpaid assessment does not stop you selling or refinancing — it stays with the lot and transfers with the required notice.

What to ask the builder before you sign

Seven questions. Get the answers in writing, during your option period, before the contract is binding:

  1. Is this lot inside a PID? Inside a MUD? Both?
  2. Give me the service and assessment plan for this district.
  3. What is the total assessment on this lot, and what is the annual installment?
  4. How many years remain on the assessment term?
  5. Is the installment collected on the county tax bill or billed separately?
  6. Can it be prepaid, and what is the current payoff?
  7. Where is the recorded notice for this lot?

Then send the answers to me, not just to your agent, so the installment is inside your qualifying numbers from the start.

What does this look like with real numbers?

This summer I worked with a couple buying a $450,000 new build in the Prosper corridor. They had a pre-approval from another lender, issued without a PID in it, and they were shopping right at the top of it.

Their builder contract disclosed a PID. The service and assessment plan put the annual installment near $1,800 — about $150 a month — running more than twenty years. Nobody had put that into their ratios.

We rebuilt the file. With 5% down, a $427,500 loan at 7.24% came to about $2,913 in principal and interest. Collin County taxes at roughly 1.5% added about $562, insurance about $185, mortgage insurance about $150, and the PID installment another $150 — a payment near $3,960. That cleared their ratios, but only just, and it was about $150 a month more than the other lender's letter had led them to budget.

Two things I did that mattered more than the quote. I had the servicer set the initial escrow from the improved value plus the actual assessment schedule, not the vacant-lot tax bill — so they will not get a shortage letter next fall. And I showed them the buying-power table, which is why they bought at $450,000 instead of stretching to $470,000 and finding out in underwriting.

They are not paying the assessment off. They plan to be there fifteen years, and I would rather they keep the cash.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a PID assessment affect how much house I can qualify for in Texas?

Is a Texas PID assessment escrowed with my property taxes?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and it depends on the district and the servicer. Many PID assessments are collected on the county tax bill and get escrowed right along with your taxes. Others are billed separately by the district or its administrator, and then you pay them directly — but your lender still counts the monthly equivalent in your qualifying ratios. Ask two questions before closing: is this PID collected on the tax bill, and is my servicer escrowing it. The answer changes what your mortgage statement looks like but not what you owe. I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and on new construction this is the single most common payment shock I see, and a PID can be part of it. Your initial escrow is often set from the most recent tax bill — which on a new build may reflect the vacant lot and may not include a PID assessment that was levied after that bill was issued. What if the seller or builder never gave me the PID notice?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and Texas gives you real remedies, though the details depend on exactly when the notice came. The notice is required before you sign a binding contract.
