How to Qualify for a Mortgage Using Your 401(k) or IRA in Texas: Fannie Mae's 2026 Asset Rules Explained

Income & QualifyingBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 14, 20266 min read

A lot of the retirees and early retirees I work with in McKinney, Allen and Rockwall have the same problem. They have $400,000 to $1 million in IRAs and 401(k)s but not much monthly income on paper, so an online calculator says they can't afford a house they could easily pay cash for. Fannie Mae has a rule for this: Employment-Related Assets as Qualifying Income (Selling Guide B3-3.4-06, updated March 4, 2026). It lets a lender convert retirement balances into monthly qualifying income without you withdrawing anything. I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329). Here's how the calculation works, its limits, and when it does and doesn't help.

Can I use my 401(k) or IRA to qualify for a mortgage?

Yes. On a conventional loan sold to Fannie Mae, the lender can take your eligible retirement assets, subtract certain amounts, and divide the rest by the loan term in months. That number counts as monthly income, the same as a pension. You don't have to start withdrawals, and the money stays invested.

The 2026 Fannie Mae rules:

RequirementFannie Mae B3-3.4-06 (2026)
Eligible assets401(k), IRA, SEP or Keogh with unrestricted access (you could withdraw everything, even with a penalty); a non-self-employed severance or lump-sum retirement package
Not eligibleOrdinary checking/savings (unless funded by one of the above), stock options, non-vested stock, inheritance, lawsuit or lottery proceeds, home sale proceeds, divorce proceeds, virtual currency

OccupancyPrincipal residence or second home (not investment property)
Income historyNone required; continuance is built into the calculation
DocumentationMost recent account statement showing holdings; evidence the funds are liquid and available

How is retirement asset income calculated?

The formula is:

(Eligible assets − early-withdrawal penalty − funds used for down payment, closing costs and reserves) ÷ loan term in months = monthly qualifying income

Three details that competitor pages often get wrong:

  • Only the penalty is subtracted, not income tax. If you're under 59½, the lender subtracts the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. It doesn't subtract the income tax you'd owe on a withdrawal.
  • No more 30% haircut. Older guidance reduced stock and mutual fund balances by 30%. Fannie Mae dropped that in 2021, so the full value counts (less the penalty).
  • On a 30-year loan the divisor is 360. That's why the income figure is smaller than people expect: $1 million produces about $2,778 a month.

Fannie Mae's own example: a $500,000 IRA held by a borrower under 59½, less a 10% penalty ($50,000), leaves $450,000. Divided by 360, that's $1,250 a month.

What does this look like for a McKinney buyer?

Here's a composite of a recent file. A couple in their mid-60s sold their Plano home and wanted a $450,000 single-story home in McKinney near their grandchildren. He's 66 and his IRA holds $640,000. Their combined Social Security is $3,900 a month. They put 40% down ($180,000) plus closing costs, all from the home sale, none from the IRA.

  • Loan: $270,000 conventional, 60% LTV, well under the 80% limit for an asset owner 62+
  • PITI: P&I about $1,869 + Collin County taxes ~1.5% ($563) + insurance $250 + HOA $50 = $2,732/mo
  • Without asset income: Social Security grossed up to $4,046, so DTI = 67.5%. Declined.
  • Asset income: $640,000 − about $16,400 set aside as six months of reserves = $623,600 ÷ 360 = $1,732/mo
  • With asset income: $4,046 + $1,732 = $5,778, so DTI = 47.3%. Approve/eligible.

They didn't withdraw anything from the IRA, and their required minimum distributions will continue on schedule. If you already take a regular monthly IRA distribution, that's often simpler and can count for more than the ÷360 figure. Lenders can't combine the two methods on the same dollars, so I run both and use whichever qualifies you better. You can also use some accounts for distributions and others for asset income, as long as no dollar is counted twice.

What should I do before applying?

  1. Don't move retirement money around before you apply. Large transfers create paperwork, and checking or savings accounts aren't eligible unless the money came from a retirement or severance distribution.
  2. Decide where the down payment comes from. Every dollar of down payment, closing costs and reserves taken from the IRA is subtracted before the division. Use home-sale proceeds or savings for the down payment if you can.
  3. Gather your latest statements for every account, showing holdings.
  4. Watch the LTV limit. If you're under 62, plan on at least 30% down.
  5. Talk to your tax advisor about withdrawals. Qualifying with assets is an underwriting calculation. It doesn't decide how you should actually pay the mortgage.

Most of my retiree clients end up on a conventional loan, and asset income is often what gets them approved. I can calculate it in a short call. See also how DTI works for Texas mortgages.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I qualify for a mortgage using my IRA or 401(k) without withdrawing money?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), a DFW mortgage loan officer, and yes. Fannie Mae's Employment-Related Assets rule (B3-3.4-06) lets a lender take your eligible 401(k), IRA, SEP or Keogh balance, subtract any early-withdrawal penalty and the money used for down payment, closing costs and reserves, and divide the rest by the loan term in months. On a 30-year loan, $500,000 of net assets counts as about $1,389 a month of qualifying income, and you don't have to start withdrawals. The loan is limited to 70% LTV, or 80% if the asset owner is 62 or older at closing. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and I'll run your numbers.

How do lenders calculate asset depletion income for a mortgage?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329) and under Fannie Mae's 2026 guide the formula is eligible retirement assets, minus the penalty for a full distribution if you're under 59½, minus funds used for down payment, closing costs and required reserves, divided by the loan's amortization term in months, which is 360 on a 30-year loan. Income taxes on a withdrawal aren't subtracted, and the old 30% haircut on stocks and mutual funds no longer applies. Fannie Mae's own example turns a $500,000 IRA with a 10% penalty into $1,250 a month. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 to calculate yours.

What is the maximum LTV when using retirement assets as income?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329) and Fannie Mae caps these loans at 70% loan-to-value, combined LTV and HCLTV, rising to 80% when the owner of the assets is at least 62 at closing. The loan must be a purchase or a limited cash-out refinance, on a principal residence or second home. If you're under 62, plan on at least 30% down; at 62 and older, 20% down works. Jointly owned assets require every owner to be a borrower. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and we'll figure out the right down payment.

Is asset depletion better than using my IRA distributions as income?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329) and it depends on what you're already doing. If you take a regular monthly IRA distribution and the account can support it for at least three years, that distribution often counts for more than the divide-by-360 asset figure. If you're not taking distributions, or don't want to start, the asset method lets you qualify without changing your withdrawal plan. Lenders can't count the same dollars both ways, so I calculate both and use whichever gives you the stronger approval. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and I'll compare them.
