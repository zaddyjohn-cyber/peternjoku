Using Your 401(k) for a Down Payment in Texas: Loan vs. Withdrawal vs. DPA (2026)

Down PaymentBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 22, 20266 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and a question I hear more often lately is "Can I use my 401(k) for the down payment?" With the 30-year rate at 7.40% (Freddie Mac, mid-October 2026) and DFW prices holding steady, many first-time buyers have more saved in retirement accounts than in checking. The answer is yes, but how you take the money matters a lot, both for taxes and for how your lender reads your file.

One quick note on the news: a bill called the Home Savings Act (H.R. 7185) was introduced in January 2026 to allow penalty-free 401(k) withdrawals for home purchases. It hasn't passed. Everything below reflects current law.

The IRA "first-time buyer" rule is broader than the name suggests. You qualify if you haven't owned a primary home in the past two years.

What does it actually cost to use $15,000 from my 401(k)?

Say you're a 32-year-old in the 22% federal bracket and need $15,000 for a down payment and closing costs. Texas has no state income tax, which helps:

In this example a hardship withdrawal costs about $7,000 in tax and penalty just to net $15,000, before counting the lost investment growth. A 401(k) loan avoids that, but the repayment comes out of your paycheck alongside a new mortgage. Interest goes back into your own account, but you're still paying about $304 a month for five years.

Does a 401(k) loan count against my debt-to-income ratio?

The sources online disagree on this, so here's how it works in practice. Under agency guidelines, loans secured by your own financial assets, like a 401(k) loan, generally don't have to be counted as a monthly debt in your DTI, because you're repaying your own account. Two caveats:

  • The money you borrowed can't also be counted as reserves, and your remaining vested balance is reduced by the loan.
  • Some lenders apply stricter overlays, and the payroll deduction still lowers your take-home pay, which affects your real budget even if it doesn't affect the ratio.

I check the loan terms on every file. If a 401(k) loan is what gets you to closing, it's usually very workable. Learn more about ratios in my DTI guide.

What documents will my lender need for 401(k) funds?

  1. Your most recent 401(k) statement showing the vested balance
  2. The plan's loan terms or withdrawal confirmation
  3. Proof the money arrived in your bank account (a bank statement or transfer record)
  4. For a withdrawal, confirmation of the net amount after tax withholding

Start early. Plan administrators can take 5 to 10 business days to send funds, and your Texas earnest money is due within a few days of signing the contract. If your down payment comes from your 401(k), file the request as soon as you're under contract, or even before, once you're pre-approved.

Is there a better option than my 401(k) in Texas?

For many first-time buyers, yes. Before touching retirement money, check whether you qualify for Texas down payment assistance. TSAHC and TDHCA programs generally provide 3–5% of the loan as a grant or a deferred, forgivable second lien. Income limits are roughly $97,200 in Dallas and Tarrant counties and $119,700 in Collin, Denton and Rockwall counties for 2026, so many buyers qualify.

On a $280,000 FHA purchase, 5% assistance on the loan is about $13,700, enough to cover the 3.5% down payment ($9,800) and part of the closing costs. That's money you never repay if it's a grant, compared with $15,000 coming out of your retirement savings. Assistance usually comes with a slightly higher rate, so I compare both on paper. But it's often the better first choice, and the two can be combined, for example DPA for the down payment and a small 401(k) loan for closing costs.

A Garland example: using two sources

Last spring I worked with a Garland couple, both in their early thirties, with credit in the 680s, buying a $289,000 home on an FHA loan. They had $6,000 in savings and about $38,000 in one spouse's 401(k). Their first plan was a $16,000 hardship withdrawal. I showed them it would have cost roughly $7,500 in taxes and penalties.

Instead, they qualified for a TSAHC grant under the Dallas County income limit, which covered most of their down payment. They used their own $6,000 and a $5,000 401(k) loan for the rest of the closing costs and reserves. The 401(k) loan payment of about $100 a month wasn't counted in their DTI, and they paid nothing in taxes or penalties.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use my 401(k) for a down payment on a house in Texas?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and yes. You can take a 401(k) loan, which avoids taxes and the 10% penalty as long as you repay it, or a hardship withdrawal if your plan allows one, which is taxed as income and usually penalized before age 59½. Lenders need your plan statement, the loan or withdrawal paperwork, and proof the funds reached your bank account. I usually check Texas down payment assistance first, since a grant doesn't cost you anything. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 to compare your options.

Does a 401(k) loan affect my mortgage approval?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329). Under agency guidelines, a loan secured by your own retirement account generally doesn't have to be counted as a monthly debt in your debt-to-income ratio, but the borrowed money can't also count as reserves, and some lenders add stricter rules. The repayment still comes out of your paycheck, so I make sure your budget works with both payments. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and I'll review your plan terms before you borrow.

How much does it cost to withdraw from a 401(k) to buy a house?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329). For a buyer under 59½ in the 22% federal bracket, a hardship withdrawal is taxed as income plus a 10% penalty, so netting $15,000 takes roughly $22,000 out of the account. Texas has no state income tax, but that's still about $7,000 gone for good, plus lost growth. A 401(k) loan or Texas down payment assistance is usually cheaper. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and I'll run your exact numbers.

Can first-time buyers withdraw from an IRA without a penalty?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and yes. A first-time buyer, meaning anyone who hasn't owned a primary home in the past two years, can withdraw up to $10,000 over their lifetime from an IRA without the 10% penalty. Traditional IRA withdrawals are still taxed as income, while Roth contributions come out tax-free. This rule applies to IRAs, not 401(k) plans.
