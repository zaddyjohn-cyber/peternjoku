Texas Mortgage Lien Release After Payoff: What Homeowners Must Know in 2026

Texas LawBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 4, 20267 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), a Texas mortgage loan officer based in Garland. One of the more common headaches I see when buyers are selling their current home to purchase a new one — or when homeowners refinance — is the discovery of an unreleased mortgage lien from a loan that was paid off years ago. Before September 1, 2023, Texas had no statute requiring a lender to formally release a lien after payoff. House Bill 219, signed into law and codified as Texas Finance Code Section 343.108, changed that. Here's what the law requires, why it matters, and what you should do if your lender hasn't complied.

What does Texas law require after a mortgage is paid off?

Under Texas Finance Code Section 343.108 (effective September 1, 2023), after your mortgage loan is paid in full, your servicer or lender must:

  • Deliver a release of lien to you, or file the release in the real property records of the county where the property is located — within 60 days of payoff.
  • If you submit a written request for the lien release within 20 days of payoff, the servicer must comply on an expedited basis — delivering or filing the release within 30 days of receiving your written request.

This matters because title searches — which every mortgage lender and title company performs before closing — will flag any unreleased lien on your property. If you're selling a home and the prior lender never filed a release, your closing cannot proceed until that lien is cleared. Tracking down a servicer from 5 years ago, proving the payoff, and waiting for them to file the release can take 30–60 days — or longer if the servicer has changed hands or merged.

What is the difference between a satisfaction of mortgage and a release of lien in Texas?

The terms are sometimes used interchangeably, but there's a technical distinction in Texas practice. A satisfaction of mortgage (also called a "payoff satisfaction") is a document the lender issues confirming the debt is paid. A release of lien is the document that must be recorded in the county property records to officially remove the lender's claim against the property. The lien release is what matters for clear title. A satisfaction document sitting in your filing cabinet but never recorded at the county is worth little in a title search — the title company looks at the recorded public records, not your personal files.

How do I verify my lien was released in DFW county records?

Every DFW county has a public online records portal where you can search for recorded instruments against your property. Here's where to look:

If you paid off your Texas mortgage since September 2023 and more than 60 days have passed without a recorded release, the servicer may be in violation of the statute. Look for a document filed by the lender or servicer — not just a letter in your files — with an instrument type of "Release of Deed of Trust," "Release of Lien," or "Satisfaction of Mortgage."

What should I do if my lender hasn't released the lien?

If you've passed the 60-day window and the lien release doesn't appear in county records, here are the steps:

  1. Send a formal written demand letter to your servicer's loss mitigation or payoff department, citing Texas Finance Code 343.108. State your loan number, payoff date, property address, and the county where the property is located. Request they file the release within 30 days.
  2. File a complaint with TDSML — the Texas Department of Savings and Mortgage Lending (sml.texas.gov) handles consumer complaints about mortgage servicers. They have regulatory authority over the servicer.
  3. Contact a Texas real estate attorney if the servicer continues to be unresponsive or has been acquired by another company. They can pursue the release through the courts and potentially recover damages.
  4. Contact your title company if you are under contract to sell. They can sometimes facilitate the release through their own legal channels to protect the closing date.

A composite example of how this plays out: A homeowner in Mesquite paid off her mortgage in full in August 2023 — just before the new law took effect. In 2026 she went under contract to sell, and the title search uncovered an unreleased deed of trust from the original servicer, which had since been acquired by another company. The title company had to track down the successor servicer, obtain a payoff verification, and wait for the release to be filed — a process that took 38 days and nearly killed the deal. The buyers extended the contract, but she lost two weeks of rent-free occupancy she'd planned on. Under today's law, this scenario would have been resolved in 2023 if she'd sent a timely written request.

How does an unreleased lien affect a cash-out refinance or new mortgage in Texas?

Any mortgage transaction — refinance, cash-out, or a new purchase where you're selling the prior home — requires a clean title search. An unreleased lien from a prior lender will appear in the title search and your title company cannot issue a clear title commitment until it's resolved. For a Texas 50(a)(6) cash-out refinance, the consequences are particularly significant because Texas home equity lending is strictly regulated — a clouded title can kill the deal entirely until the lien is cleared.

If you're approaching a refinance and you've paid off a prior mortgage in the last 1–5 years, it takes about 5 minutes to check your county records online and confirm the release was filed. Do it before you apply. If it's missing, start the written demand process immediately — it runs parallel to the loan application and usually resolves before the underwriting is complete, but only if you start early.
