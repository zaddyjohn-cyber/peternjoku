HistoryMaker Homes Sold to Century Communities: What DFW Buyers Under Contract Should Ask About Their Mortgage

New ConstructionBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 20, 20268 min read

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland, and since October 8, 2026 I've had the same question from three different buyers: "My HistoryMaker home isn't finished yet — what happens to my loan now that Century Communities bought the company?" It's a fair question. HistoryMaker started in Fort Worth in 1949, builds only in Dallas–Fort Worth, and has finished about 25,000 homes. Century Communities, a publicly traded national builder, bought substantially all of the assets of HMH Lifestyles, L.P. (the company that operates as HistoryMaker Homes). Century says the deal roughly triples its DFW community count.

The news coverage so far — Builder, Dallas Express, the press release reposts — is written for investors and the building industry. None of it tells a buyer under contract what to check about their financing. That's the gap I'm filling here: what the announcement actually promised, what it didn't address, and the questions I'd put in writing before your closing date.

What did Century Communities announce about HistoryMaker buyers?

Here's what Century and HistoryMaker said publicly in the October 8 announcement and the coverage that followed:

  • Contracts carry on. Construction, sales and closings are continuing on schedule, and buyers already under contract keep working with their local HistoryMaker team.
  • One combined division. Century is merging its existing DFW operation with HistoryMaker into a single division based in Grapevine, and HistoryMaker's division leaders and staff are moving over to Century.
  • Price not disclosed. Neither company disclosed the purchase price or the exact number of communities and lots involved.
  • Sister companies stay separate. Jabez Development and ONM Living (a build-to-rent company) remain independently owned. Jabez will keep doing land development work in select Century communities.

Here's what the announcement did not address: home prices, buyer incentives, which lender closes HistoryMaker loans going forward, or how warranties will be administered. That's not a red flag — acquisitions are usually announced first and the operating details get worked out later. But "not addressed" is exactly where a buyer with a closing date in November or December should ask questions.

Does the acquisition change my mortgage if I'm already under contract?

Your mortgage is between you and your lender, not between you and the builder. If you're pre-approved or already in underwriting, the sale of the builder's assets doesn't cancel your loan application, your rate lock, or your appraisal. What can change is everything around the loan that the builder controls:

That last row is not a throwaway. Any time a transaction has a change of company, new email addresses and new staff, it's an opening for wire fraud. If you get an email saying "our title office has changed, here are new wiring instructions," call the title company at a number you looked up yourself before you send a dollar. I wrote a whole post on protecting your closing funds from wire fraud — read it before closing week.

Will I have to switch to Century's mortgage company?

Century Communities has its own affiliated mortgage company, Inspire Home Loans, and its online buying process for Century homes routes buyers to Inspire after they sign. Nothing I've found says HistoryMaker's existing buyers are being moved to Inspire, and nothing says they aren't. So here's how the rules work regardless of who owns the builder:

  • A builder can't force you to use its affiliated lender. Under RESPA's affiliated business rules, a builder that refers you to a company it has an ownership interest in has to give you an Affiliated Business Arrangement disclosure, and generally can't require you to use that affiliate as a condition of the sale.
  • A builder can offer an incentive for using its lender. That's legal and common in DFW new construction. The incentive is a price you're paying for convenience — if the outside lender's rate and fees beat the builder package even after the incentive, the outside lender is cheaper.
  • Read your contract's incentive language. If your HistoryMaker contract says the incentive applies "when using [named lender]," ask in writing whether that still holds after the acquisition, and get the answer from the builder, not a verbal assurance from a salesperson.

I compare builder-lender packages to my own pricing all the time — see my breakdown of builder preferred lender vs. an outside lender. The short version: compare the Loan Estimates line by line, and compare the total cost over the years you'll keep the loan, not just the incentive headline.

What happens to my rate lock if the closing date slips?

This is the risk I watch most closely. Freddie Mac's survey put the 30-year fixed at 7.40% for the week of October 15, 2026 — unchanged from the week before and the highest since November 2023. If your lock expires before the house is finished, you either pay to extend or re-lock at whatever the market is that day.

A HistoryMaker buyer I'm working with right now

A couple I'm working with — first-time buyers, combined income around $118,000, credit scores in the low 700s — signed on a HistoryMaker home in the Fort Worth area in August at $389,000, with an estimated completion in mid-December. Their builder incentive was a closing-cost credit. When the acquisition news broke, they called me worried their loan was "in limbo."

It wasn't. Their application, credit and income documentation were all still good. What we did was three things: (1) they emailed their HistoryMaker sales contact asking for written confirmation of the incentive amount and whether it depended on a particular lender; (2) we checked the completion estimate against their lock and chose a lock period that runs past the estimated date with a cushion, rather than a short lock they'd have to extend; and (3) I put the title company's main number in their file, looked up independently, so nobody could swap wire instructions on them in December. Total time: one afternoon. Their closing is still on track.

Should I buy a HistoryMaker or Century home now, or wait?

The acquisition by itself isn't a reason to walk away from a contract or to rush into one. What matters is the same math as any new build: the price compared to resale homes nearby, the incentive in real dollars, the HOA and any MUD tax in the community, and whether your payment works at today's rate. Two things are worth knowing in October 2026:

  • Builders are motivated in Q4. Year-end is when builders push to close inventory homes. See my notes on DFW new-construction incentives and how a 2-1 buydown on new construction compares to a price cut.
  • Integration periods can bring promotions. When a national builder takes over a local one, it sometimes runs promotions to move existing inventory. That's speculation on my part, not something Century has announced — but it's a reason to ask what incentives are available today before you sign.

How do I get my financing ready for a DFW new build?

Get a full pre-approval with income and asset documentation, not just a credit pull, before you sign a builder contract. Ask for your lock options at the start — a 60-, 90- or 120-day lock costs more upfront than a 30-day lock, but it can be cheaper than repeated extensions on a home that's months from completion. If you're in Fort Worth, Grapevine or anywhere else on the west side, my Fort Worth page has local loan details; on the east side, start with Garland. And if you want a second set of eyes on a builder's Loan Estimate, send it to me — I'll tell you honestly whether the builder package is the better deal.
