Using a Power of Attorney at a Texas Mortgage Closing: The Rule That Trips Everyone Up

Closing ProcessBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 30, 20269 min read

Roughly once a month a buyer tells me, usually about ten days before closing, that they will be out of the country on the signing date and their brother or spouse will "just sign for them." In most states that is a routine piece of paperwork. In Texas it is a rule set that almost nobody outside a title company or a lender's compliance department has read — and one of those rules governs something no other state regulates: where the power of attorney itself gets signed.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland (NMLS #2670329). The pages that rank for this question in Texas are a brokerage blog, a title company and two law firms. They are decent on what the document must say. What none of them covers is the lender layer — which loan types will accept a power of attorney, which effectively won't, and the Texas regulation that voids an otherwise perfect document because of the room it was signed in. That is what follows.

Can I close on a Texas mortgage with a power of attorney?

Usually yes on a purchase. Often no on a home equity loan. And in every case the answer belongs to your lender, not to you or your attorney.

A power of attorney lets someone you name — your attorney-in-fact — sign the closing documents on your behalf. Three separate parties have to accept it: the lender, the title company, and for VA and USDA loans the agency. Any one of them can refuse. The lender's approval has to come before the document is drafted, because most lenders want their own template rather than one your attorney prefers, and a document you have already signed is far harder to fix than one not yet written.

So the first move is never "have a POA drawn up." It is "tell the loan officer."

Where does Texas law say the power of attorney has to be signed?

This is the rule that catches people, and I have never once seen it on a consumer page.

7 Texas Administrative Code §153.15 governs the location of closing for a Section 50(a)(6) home equity loan. It does two things:

  1. A Texas home equity loan may close only at an office of the lender, an attorney at law, or a title company. Not your kitchen table, not a coffee shop, not a notary's house.
  2. Any power of attorney allowing an attorney-in-fact to execute those closing documents must be signed by the owner, or the owner's spouse, at the permanent physical address of an office of the lender, an attorney at law, or a title company.

Read that second one twice. The regulation reaches backwards in time to the moment your power of attorney was executed. A beautifully drafted, properly notarized, transaction-specific power of attorney signed at a bank branch in Ohio does not satisfy it — a bank branch is not the office of the lender on your loan, an attorney at law, or a title company.

You also have to be able to prove where it was signed. The regulation accepts three forms of evidence:

  • a written statement inside the power of attorney acknowledging the date and place it was executed;
  • an affidavit from a witness who was present at the signing; or
  • a notary certificate of acknowledgment that states the date and place of signing.

A 2020 amendment loosened the geography slightly in a way I find genuinely useful: the closing may occur in any area at that permanent physical address — an indoor office or the parking lot. So a curbside signing outside the title company counts. A signing twenty miles away does not.

Practically, compliance guidance is blunt about the consequence: unless the lender has verification that the power of attorney was executed in the proper location, it should not be used to close the home equity loan. Which is why many Texas title companies decline them on 50(a)(6) files as a matter of policy rather than argue about proof.

Why is a Texas home equity loan the hardest case?

Because our constitution stacks protections that all assume the owner is personally present and personally informed. A 50(a)(6) already carries a 12-day notice before closing, an 80% loan-to-value ceiling, a required written acknowledgment of fair market value, and a three-day right of rescission afterwards. Then §153.15 adds the office-only closing and the signing-location rule for a power of attorney.

Put those together and a lender is being asked to rely on an absent owner's agent to receive constitutionally mandated disclosures and exercise a constitutionally mandated cancellation right. Most won't. I have seen one aged compliance summary assert that Fannie Mae will not purchase a Texas home equity loan closed by power of attorney at all — I could not verify that against the Selling Guide, so I won't state it as fact, but it tells you the direction of the industry's caution. Either way, the practical answer is the same: get it in writing from the lender before you plan around it. My Texas cash-out guide walks through the rest of the 50(a)(6) rules.

What has to be in the document for the title company to accept it?

Separate from where it is signed, the content has to be right. A general durable power of attorney — the kind in most estate-planning binders — is almost never sufficient for a Texas real estate closing. What title companies want to see:

  • Transaction-specific authority. Not "all real estate matters" but this purchase or this refinance.
  • The property identified by legal description, or at minimum the full street address.
  • The exact scope spelled out — which documents the attorney-in-fact may execute, including the note and the deed of trust by name.
  • Notarized, and recorded in the county where the property sits. Texas Property Code §12.001 governs recording instruments affecting real property; Texas practitioners also point to the Estates Code for the timing on recording a durable power of attorney used with a recordable instrument. Your title company will tell you exactly when and where it files.
  • Not a "springing" power of attorney. One that takes effect only on your incapacity is useless for a closing where you are perfectly competent and merely elsewhere.
  • Recently dated. There is no statutory expiration for a Texas power of attorney, but in practice underwriters get uncomfortable with one much older than six months, and many will ask for a fresh one.

Two more rules worth knowing. Remote online notarization has been available in Texas since 2019, which solves the practical problem for a deployed servicemember or an overseas seller — but it does not solve §153.15, which is about location, not about the method of notarization. And under TREC Rule 535.16 your real estate agent cannot act as your attorney-in-fact on a transaction where they represent you. Do not name your Realtor.

My spouse isn't on the loan — can they sign by power of attorney?

Expect a fight on this one, and understand why the signature is needed in the first place.

On Texas homestead property, a non-borrowing spouse generally has to sign the security instrument regardless of whether they are on the loan or on title. They are not becoming a borrower; they are joining in to release homestead rights. The requirement comes from homestead law, not from community property — a point that even some lender pages get wrong — with an exception for purchase-money mortgages. A prenuptial agreement does not override it. I go deeper in my post on the non-borrowing spouse rules.

So if your spouse is the one who will be absent, their joinder has to be executed by an attorney-in-fact — which must satisfy the lender's policy and, on a home equity loan, §153.15's requirement that the owner's spouse sign the power of attorney at one of those three offices. Note that the regulation names the spouse explicitly. Some lenders decline spousal powers of attorney entirely. Find out which kind yours is early.

What does this look like with real numbers?

Earlier this year I worked with an active-duty buyer stationed away from Texas, purchasing a $320,000 home in Fort Worth on a VA loan with zero down. His wife was handling the closing locally.

We flagged the power of attorney in our first phone call, nineteen days before his contract was even signed. That one decision is why this story is boring. The lender issued its template in four days. VA review came back in nine business days. He executed at a title company office near his duty station — which satisfied §153.15's location requirement even though it was not the title company handling our file — with the notary certificate stating the date and place. Title recorded it, approved it, and we closed on the original date.

His loan came to roughly $320,000. At 7.24% — the Freddie Mac survey average for the week of October 29, 2026, down from 7.32% the prior week and 6.17% a year earlier — principal and interest ran about $2,181 a month. With Tarrant County taxes near 2.2% (about $587 a month), insurance around $195, and no monthly mortgage insurance on a VA loan, his payment landed close to $2,963. The power of attorney cost him a notary fee, a recording fee and about $40 of overnight shipping.

Compare that to the file I inherited two years ago where the buyer had already signed a general durable power of attorney before anyone asked, at a credit union in another state, with no statement of place. We lost eleven days and the seller charged a per-diem.
