Mineral Rights and Oil & Gas Leases on a Texas Mortgage: Will It Stop Your Closing?

Title & ClosingBy Bond Peter NjokuOctober 30, 20268 min read

Let me answer the question in the first paragraph, because the pages that rank for it don't. A severed mineral estate or a recorded oil and gas lease on your title commitment almost never stops a DFW residential closing. I have closed loans on houses with 1940s mineral reservations, 1980s oil and gas leases, and pooling agreements in the chain. They closed on time.

I'm Bond Peter Njoku, a mortgage loan officer in Garland (NMLS #2670329). The reason buyers panic about this is the search results. Google "mineral rights Texas mortgage" and you get a title underwriting manual behind a sign-in, a continuing-education deck for title attorneys, a law firm article written for lender's counsel, and a REALTOR association legal FAQ. All useful to professionals. Not one of them answers "will this stop my closing?" So here is that answer, with the lender and title-policy detail those pages leave out.

First, the vocabulary — in plain English

In Texas the surface estate and the mineral estate are separate pieces of property that can be owned by different people. When they get split, that is a severance, and severances in Texas go back a century in places. Three words you will see:

  • A reservation is the seller keeping something — "seller reserves all of the mineral estate." It happens in your transaction.
  • An exception is the title company declining to insure something that already happened — a severance by a prior owner, or a recorded lease. It shows up on Schedule B of your commitment.
  • An oil and gas lease is an agreement letting an operator explore and produce. A recorded one appearing as an exception is often just evidence that a severance occurred earlier in the chain.

Two more: a royalty is a share of production without the cost of producing it — classically one-eighth. A bonus is the up-front payment for signing a lease, sometimes as small as a few hundred dollars on a residential lot. Neither is yours if the minerals were severed before you bought.

And the one that worries people: a mineral owner generally holds an implied easement to use the surface to the extent reasonably necessary to extract the minerals. True as a doctrine. Almost never consequential on a subdivision lot, for reasons I'll get to.

What does my title policy actually cover on minerals?

Less than buyers assume, and the gap is filled by an endorsement most people have never heard of. No consumer page in this SERP mentions either form.

What happens
Mineral ownership itselfStandard Texas policies exclude coverage of mineral ownership and make no affirmative statement about who owns what underground
A recorded oil and gas leaseListed as a Schedule B exception — and a useful clue that a severance happened earlier
T-19.2 Minerals and Surface Damage endorsementResidential of one acre or less and commercial. Covers damage to improvements from surface use for mineral development, excluding lawns, shrubbery and trees
T-19.3 Minerals and Surface Damage endorsementAll other property. Narrower — limited to permanent buildings
Promulgated premium$50.00 on an Owner's Policy, $0.00 on a Loan Policy — issued per tract, so multiple tracts can mean multiple endorsements
What the endorsements do NOT doThey do not give you the minerals, do not stop anyone from producing them, and do not cover loss of the mineral estate — only surface damage from mineral development

Two things stand out. T-19.2 is actually the broader improvement coverage even though the number is lower, because it reaches improvements generally while T-19.3 stops at permanent buildings — so on a typical DFW lot of an acre or less you want the one you qualify for anyway. And the lender's endorsement is free. Ask for both. Fifty dollars is the cheapest line item on your settlement statement; the published rate structure for the rest is in my guide to Texas title insurance costs.

Get the actual endorsement wording from your title company rather than my summary — the forms have specific definitions and I would rather you read them.

What do Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac require?

This is the part that decides whether your loan closes, and it is absent from every page ranking above this one.

Fannie MaeFreddie Mac
Where the rule livesSelling Guide B7-2-05, Title Exceptions and ImpedimentsSeller/Servicer Guide title requirements
The standardOutstanding oil, water or mineral rights are acceptable where customarily waived by other lendersNo right of surface or subsurface entry within 200 feet of a residence
Added conditionMust not materially alter the contour of the property or impair its value or usefulnessA title policy endorsement is required to protect the lender
Practical effect in DFWRoutine exceptions pass without commentThe 200-foot setback is the question an underwriter actually asks

Two cautions, because these numbers get mangled online. The 200-foot rule is Freddie's, not Fannie's — don't let anyone tell you Fannie has a setback. And Texas law separately holds that "minerals" excludes coal, lignite and iron ore on or within 200 feet of the surface where producing them would destroy or deplete the surface. Same number, completely unrelated doctrine. Don't merge them. (Fannie's multifamily guide uses other distances entirely — 250 feet subsurface, 600 feet for equipment on adjacent property. Those are multifamily rules and have nothing to do with your house.)

When does a mineral issue actually threaten a closing?

Rarely, and the cases are identifiable in advance. In order of how often I see them:

  1. Visible production near the house. An active wellhead, a tank battery, a compressor — close enough that Freddie's 200-foot line is in play, or close enough that the appraiser comments on marketability. This is the one that generates real conditions.
  2. Surface use not waived. Most residential severances in built-out areas come with a waiver of surface rights, which is why nobody notices them. A reservation that expressly keeps surface access is a different conversation.
  3. A pipeline or pooling easement crossing the building envelope. Here the problem is the survey, not the minerals — an easement running under the house or the garage. See my T-47 survey post for how survey exceptions get handled.
  4. Acreage and the edge of development. On a few acres outside city limits, the reasonably-necessary surface easement stops being theoretical. This is also where T-19.3's narrower coverage applies instead of T-19.2's.
  5. An appraiser's adverse-influence note. Not a title problem at all, but an appraisal problem, and it reaches your underwriter the same way.

What is not on that list: an old reservation, a decades-old recorded lease with no production, a royalty interest held by someone's grandchildren. Those are exceptions, they get read, they get endorsed, and the file moves.

Why does this come up so often in Tarrant and Denton counties?

Because of the Barnett Shale. Fort Worth sits on it, and so do Denton, Johnson and Wise counties. A decade and a half of leasing activity there means title chains in those counties are full of oil and gas leases, pooling agreements and mineral deeds that a buyer in, say, Rockwall may never encounter.

And it is the reason Fort Worth-area buyers see this language and assume the worst. Two structural things keep it from becoming a loan problem inside city limits: municipal drilling ordinances, which heavily restrict where a well can be sited relative to homes, and plain geometry — a 60-foot-wide lot cannot host a drill site. The REALTOR association guidance makes the same point qualitatively: minerals have historically not been an issue in typical residential sales because the minerals may already be severed, the lots are small, and cities regulate drilling. I'd add the lender's version: the exception gets endorsed and nobody thinks about it again.

I will not give you a percentage for how much Texas property is severed. The claim that "most" of it is comes from one practitioner summary and I could not verify a figure. It is common. That is as far as the data goes.

The contract layer: how minerals get handled before you ever see a commitment

If the seller wants to keep the minerals, that happens in the contract, not at closing. In the standard Texas one-to-four family residential resale contract (TREC 20-18 / TXR 1601), paragraph 2E is where reservations are made, and they are made by attaching the Addendum for Reservation of Oil, Gas and Other Minerals (TREC 44-3 / TXR 1905). The Farm and Ranch contract (TREC 25-16 / TXR 1701) does the same thing at paragraph 6E. There is also an informational form, TXR 2509, explaining the mineral clauses.

What this means for you: if that addendum is attached to your contract, the seller is keeping all or part of the mineral estate going forward. Read it during the option period. It is not a loan problem, but it is a thing you are agreeing to.

What do I do in the seven days after I get the title commitment?

A short checklist, and the whole point is that it happens inside the option period rather than the week of closing:

  1. Open Schedule B and read every exception. Note each mineral reservation, oil and gas lease, pipeline easement and pooling agreement, with its recording date.
  2. Ask the title company one question first: has the surface use been waived in these instruments?
  3. Ask for the endorsement by name — T-19.2 if you are on an acre or less, T-19.3 otherwise — on both the owner's policy and the loan policy.
  4. Look out the window. Any visible wellhead, tank, or access road within a couple hundred feet of the house? Tell me before the appraisal is ordered.
  5. Check the survey for any easement crossing the house, garage or driveway.
  6. Send me the commitment. I would rather clear an underwriter's question in week one than negotiate it in week four.
  7. If you are buying acreage, or anything with a producing well, talk to a Texas real estate attorney. I am a loan officer, not your counsel.

What does this look like with real numbers?

Last spring I had a buyer under contract on a $330,000 house in west Fort Worth. His commitment came back with two things that alarmed him: a mineral reservation from a 1978 deed and a recorded oil and gas lease from 1984.

We spent about forty minutes on it, total. The title company confirmed the surface rights had been waived in the reservation and the 1984 lease showed no production of record. They issued a T-19.2 on both policies — $50 on his owner's policy, $0 on the lender's. The nearest wellhead was more than a mile away, well outside Freddie's 200-foot line, and the appraiser never mentioned minerals. The underwriter accepted the exceptions without a condition.

He put 10% down — a $297,000 loan. At 7.24%, the Freddie Mac survey average for the week of October 29, 2026, principal and interest came to about $2,024 a month. With Tarrant County taxes near 2.2% (about $605), insurance around $200, and mortgage insurance of roughly $120, his payment landed near $2,949. The mineral question cost him fifty dollars and a phone call.

I tell that story because the alternative version exists: the buyer who reads "oil, gas and other minerals" on page four of a commitment two days before closing, goes looking online, finds a title attorney's CLE deck, and calls me at nine at night.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a mortgage on a Texas house if I don't own the mineral rights?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and yes — in the overwhelming majority of cases this is a non-event. Mineral severance is common across Texas, and lenders see reserved mineral estates and old oil and gas leases on title commitments every day. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide treats outstanding oil, water or mineral rights as an acceptable exception where they are customarily waived by other lenders and don't materially alter the contour of the property or impair its value or usefulness. Freddie Mac goes further and wants no right of surface or subsurface entry within 200 feet of the residence, plus a title endorsement protecting the lender. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 and send me Schedule B.

Will my lender make me buy a minerals endorsement in Texas?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and often the lender will want one — and here is the good news: in Texas the promulgated premium for the Minerals and Surface Damage endorsements, forms T-19.2 and T-19.3, is $50 on an owner's policy and $0 on a loan policy, issued per tract. So the lender's protection costs nothing and your own costs fifty dollars. T-19.2 applies to residential property of one acre or less and to commercial; T-19.3 covers everything else. They insure against damage from surface use for mineral development — they do not give you the minerals. Ask your title company for the actual endorsement language. Call or text me at 469-545-7180.

Can someone drill in my backyard if they own the minerals under my house?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and in theory a mineral owner usually holds an implied easement to use the surface to the extent reasonably necessary to extract the minerals — which sounds alarming and almost never plays out on a quarter-acre DFW lot. Two things protect you in practice: city drilling ordinances inside municipal limits, which heavily restrict where a well can go, and the simple geometry of a subdivision lot that cannot host a drill site. Where it does matter is on acreage, on the edge of development, and where an existing well or production equipment is already visible nearby. Call or text me at 469-545-7180 if there is a wellhead in sight of the house.

What should I do if my title commitment shows an oil and gas lease?

I'm Bond Peter Njoku (NMLS #2670329), and do it during your option period, not the week of closing. Read Schedule B and note every mineral reservation, oil and gas lease, pipeline easement and pooling agreement. Ask the title company two questions: has the surface use been waived, and will you issue a T-19.2 or T-19.3 endorsement. Then send me the commitment so I can get ahead of the underwriter and, if there's visible production nearby, flag it before the appraisal is ordered. A recorded lease listed as an exception is usually just evidence that a severance happened decades ago. Call or text me at 469-545-7180.
