## Facts cross-checked (use these)
- No universal dollar cap on gift funds — the limit is about WHO can give and whether the buyer
  must contribute any of their own money, not a flat dollar ceiling.
- Conventional (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac), owner-occupied primary residence: 100% of the down payment
  can be a gift from an acceptable donor (family member) when putting down ≥20%. For less than 20%
  down on a 1-unit primary residence, 100% gift is also generally allowed under Fannie Mae's current
  guidelines (this is a common source of confusion since older rules required a minimum borrower
  contribution — flag but state current guidance, don't repeat a stale 5%-contribution rule as if
  universal).
- FHA: 100% of the down payment can be a gift from an eligible family member/donor — this is one of
  FHA's most flexible features and pairs well with TSAHC/TDHCA DPA on top.
- VA: no down payment required at all, but if a buyer chooses to put money down, the entire amount
  can be gifted, and gift funds can also cover closing costs.
- USDA: 100% financing already, but gift funds are fully allowed toward closing costs (USDA doesn't
  require a down payment, so "down payment gift" doesn't apply the same way — gift funds go toward
  closing costs instead).
- Acceptable donors: typically immediate family (parent, sibling, grandparent), sometimes a fiancé/
  domestic partner; must NOT be an interested party to the transaction (agent, builder, seller).
- Documentation: signed gift letter (no repayment expected) + proof of donor's ability to gift
  (bank statement showing funds) + proof of transfer (withdrawal from donor, deposit to
  borrower) — "sourcing and seasoning" of the gift is what underwriters actually scrutinize, not
  the dollar amount.
- 2026 annual gift tax exclusion referenced by competitors as $19,000 (2025 figure) — verify current
  IRS figure before publishing; if unchanged for 2026, cite it as such, otherwise use $19,000 with a
  disclaimer that the buyer should confirm with a tax professional (gift tax is a donor-side IRS
  issue, not a mortgage qualifying limit, and the exclusion has zero effect on how much can be
  gifted toward a house — this distinction is missing from most competitor pages).
